## Format brief — everything that isn't a single card

> The five colours are close enough that colour choice is near noise. Archetype and card-level
> differences are not.

**LSV's one-line summary of ECL: "find the open lane."** Five creature-type archetypes plus an
unwritten sixth (vivid), and effectively nothing else. It plays out **midrange and grindy rather
than fast** — which is why the format rewards creature-type density and card advantage over raw
stats, and why removal is the class the field over-drafts.

### What the data says

*(The full archetype table renders directly above this brief.)*

**The five supported pairs are the format, and it is not close.** They take the top five slots and
almost the entire field; the five unsupported pairs are all bad and all rare. This is the most
decisive archetype split in this reference, and it confirms the Rough Drafts retrospective's settled
read exactly: the official framing of Blight and Vivid as "decks" for the off-pairs led the community
astray. **They are modifiers you layer onto a supported pair, not places to be.**

**Elves is the best deck and the most-drafted deck at once** — a contested lane that wins anyway.
Then Merfolk, Kithkin and Elementals, all within a hair of each other, with Goblins a step behind.

**This is the largest expert reversal anywhere in this reference, and two channels reached it
independently.** Limited Resources opened the format with LSV calling Goblins "the strongest
archetype," confidence high — and by the Sunset Show it had finished *below the average of all ten
pairs*. He cited his own 17Lands read on air: Elves ~58%, Kithkin ~57%, Merfolk ~56%, Elementals
~55%, **Goblins ~53%**. Elves, which he had ranked third at review, became "the standout best deck of
the format, not close." **Lords of Limited's retrospective got to the same place from a separate
sample** — Elves best in the format, best in both the early and late game. Two channels converging on
the same reversal, and the finished data agreeing with both, is much stronger evidence than either
alone. LSV diagnosed his own error precisely: he drafted one busted Goblins deck in early access and
played against two more, and generalised from three games' worth of variance.

**Goblins is a one-drafter lane; Elves supports three.** That is Paul Cheon's framing on LR, and it
is the practical form of the reversal — Goblins is not unplayable, it is *depth-limited*. Its commons
are the flattest in the set, so a Goblins deck built from commons without the uncommon payoffs is a
losing deck, while an Elves deck built from commons is fine.

**Colour order: green and blue tied at the top, then white, black, and red last** — but the whole
band is narrow. Green edging it matches Rough Drafts' read that green is strongest on the depth of
its commons and uncommons rather than on the elf payoffs.

**The Eclipsed hybrid cycle is the best uncommon cycle in the set, and this is the format's most
important fact.** All five — Elf, Kithkin, Merrow, Flamekin, Boggart — land in the top fifteen
uncommons. These are precisely the *low-commitment* typal payoffs: they ask for a few creatures of a
type, not fifteen, which is why the "divorce the pair from its tribe" advice below is the right way
to draft. The one genuinely **high**-commitment elf payoff, Morcant's Eyes, is excellent too, but it
is the exception rather than the model.

**The best uncommon in the set is Deepchannel Duelist**, the Merfolk lord.

**Best commons:** Dawnhand Eulogist (which wheels), Assert Perfection, Scarblade Scout, Merrow
Skyswimmer, Lys Alana Informant, **Kulrath Zealot**, Mistmeadow Council and **Flamekin Gildweaver**.

### The draft plan

1. **Be in one of the five supported pairs.** Everything else is far worse. If you find yourself
   drifting into an unsupported pair, that is a signal you have misread the seat, not that you have
   found something clever.
2. **Take the Eclipsed hybrid for your pair very highly** — they are five of the fifteen best
   uncommons and the cheapest way to make a typal deck work.
3. **Then draft the colours, not the tribe.** You want to *end up* in a supported pair, but very few
   cards demand a high creature-type count. Most payoffs are happy with five to eight of a type. A
   Black-Green deck with some goblin cards in it is fine; a Merfolk deck running a kithkin payoff off
   changelings is fine.
4. **Draft for the density of genuinely impactful cards, not for a smooth pile of playables.** This
   is Rough Drafts' central mid-format realisation and the most actionable thing here: ECL has an
   unusual tier spread — many A-level cards, unusually few generically-good B's. The "pack your deck
   with C+/B goodstuff and out-value them" plan that works in most formats **loses** in this one. The
   uncommons and rares carry games; the commons are role players.
5. **Hold removal for the lords and the engines.** With a flat colour spread and top-heavy card
   quality, games are decided by whether their impactful card resolves and sticks — not by trading
   two damage into a two-drop.
6. **Stake the lane in the first three picks, and take the hook over the hedge.** Limited Resources'
   sharpest procedural rule: a flexible card doesn't make you draft a deck, a payoff does — take the
   card that *commits* you over the one that keeps a door open, because the format is unforgiving
   about deciding late. "You really can't wait till pack two to decide what colour pair you are."
   Note this pulls against step 3's low-commitment framing; both hosts also concede that burning
   early picks to stay open is defensible, and neither position won the argument.

### What the numbers can't tell you

- **Blight and Vivid are orthogonal vectors, not archetypes.** A single Prismabasher in a BG deck
  subtly changes what fills the back half of your deck — now the colour-count payoffs are live. A
  fully dedicated Vivid deck is a rarer, different thing. Same for blight: goblins can be a hard typal
  deck **or** a blight-burn grind deck, and those want different cards.
- **Changelings keep you open**, because they satisfy every typal payoff at once. That is what makes
  the low-commitment plan work in practice.
- **Convoke wants a genuinely low curve**, and vigilance on your attackers is load-bearing — attack
  with them, then still convoke your instant-speed spells after combat.
- **Creatures run small**, which is why a four-mana 6/6 plays as a real threat and why removal that
  scales past two damage is at a premium. It is also why Cinder Strike's blight-boosted mode matters:
  killing a four-mana 4/4 for one mana is the line.
- **The lords tax removal beyond their anthem.** Once you have played one, the opponent has to start
  respecting the next — which buys your other threats a turn.
- **High flash density punishes slamming into open mana.** Play around it more than you would in a
  format of this speed otherwise.
- **Two-drops without synergy are dead weight** — in a top-heavy format the curve effectively starts
  on three unless the two-drop is doing something.
- **It plays midrange and grindy, and that was the biggest structural surprise.** Both LR hosts
  expected Bloomburrow speed out of Kithkin, Goblins and Merfolk and got board stalls and long games
  instead. Two consequences: **flyers and evasion are premium** because they break stalls, and
  **cards with inherent two-for-ones beat cards with raw stats.** That is the mechanism behind the
  "impactful density" rule above, stated in gameplay terms.
- **But you still cannot durdle.** Every deck is a creature deck. Missing a turn-two play against
  Kithkin or Merfolk is punished immediately, so "grindy" is not permission to build a do-nothing
  curve — it is permission to build a *top-heavy* one.
- **Combat tricks as a class do not earn slots.** There is no good one-mana trick in the set, and the
  removal is good enough that holding up a trick loses you the game you were trying to win. LR name
  this as one of their whole-set misses: only Blossoming Defense clears the bar, with Gilt-Leaf's
  Embrace behind it in Elves. Everything else is a non-card.
- **Off-type cards must clear a much higher bar than "fine."** Anything that isn't your creature type
  is competing against a payoff, not against a replacement-level card, so it has to be uncommon-power
  good before it earns the slot.
- **Sealed is a different and worse format.** Pools are spikier because you need multiple good cards
  of the *same* type, and the bombs are type-gated — you cannot splash a payoff that reads "elves."
  A pool with one of each Eclipsed card is a blank; a pool with two or three of one type is a very
  good deck. Count your type before you count your rares.

### Deckbuilding doctrine

- **Creature counts run 15–20**, even in the Vivid and Blight builds.
- **Blight is upside only when you build for it.** The cards that ask you to blight are traps in a
  deck with nothing to gain from the counters.
- **Don't chase a contested tribal lane.** Being in a supported pair unlocks the gold lords, the
  Eclipsed cards and the Commands — but a *contested* lane makes your deck genuinely bad, and the
  changelings are what let you pivot cheaply.
- **Limited Resources counts creature types, not playables: 16–18 of your type**, leaving six or
  seven slots that they want to be removal and nothing else. That is a much heavier commitment than
  the low-commitment plan above, and the two readings are not reconcilable by splitting the
  difference — see Open questions.
- **Gathering Stone is the deck's self-check.** LSV: if your Elf, Merfolk, Kithkin or Goblin deck
  doesn't happily want it, you didn't draft a good version of that deck. Marshall's version is the
  useful half — if you *can* play it and feel good, your deck is fine even when the raw card quality
  isn't the highest. It is the cheapest test of whether you actually reached critical mass.
- **Blight is the set's weakest mechanic and it taxes you.** Any card asking you to shrink your own
  board has to pay you back in a major way, and most of them don't. Plus-one counters are simply a
  better game piece, which is a structural reason Goblins came in last of the five rather than a
  card-quality one.

### Where the field misprices cards

- **Removal is the class the field over-drafts, and this is the format's biggest systematic error.**
  Both LR hosts report routinely cutting good removal as their *last* cuts — Marshall cut four in one
  build — and Paul Cheon's winning shape is 18–19 creatures with only three or four removal spells.
  Games go long and grindy, so one-for-ones don't decide them, and every non-type slot costs critical
  mass. **Shift every removal spell down roughly half a grade against an on-type creature.** This
  sharpens rather than contradicts the "hold removal for the lords" rule above: take fewer, and spend
  them bigger.
- **The Eclipsed cycle was under-rated even by the people who liked it.** LR graded them B+ in their
  own pair at review and called them "A+ great, not even in the ballpark of reasonable" at the end —
  both hosts will windmill one pack 1 pick 1. Independent agreement with the data putting all five in
  the top fifteen uncommons.
- **The five unsupported pairs are a design trap, not a contrarian seat.** Combined they were about
  8% of decks drafted, and LR's settled verdict is that there is not enough there to support even one
  drafter with any consistency. The set seeds appealing off-brand cards with no archetype behind them;
  take those cards on raw rate for a supported deck, never as a plan.
- **The shocklands are the format's most expensive habit.** Temple Garden, Hallowed Fountain, Blood
  Crypt, Steam Vents and Overgrown Tomb are all taken in the first four picks and none of them does
  anything for the game you draw it in. Fixing you don't need, bought with picks you do.
- **Taken early, and shouldn't be:** **Chomping Changeling** is the worst of them, then Collective
  Inferno, Hexing Squelcher, Meanders Guide, Burdened Stoneback and Spiral into Solitude.
  **Mischievous Sneakling** deserves its own mention — a heavily-played UB common in the format's
  worst colour pair.
- **Wheels, and shouldn't:** **Dawnhand Eulogist** is the biggest gap in the set, then Flamekin
  Gildweaver, Midnight Tilling, Temporal Cleansing, **Kindle the Inner Flame**, Gravelgill Scoundrel,
  Flaring Cinder and Rooftop Percher.
- **Cinder Strike is very good, and the "best common in the format" billing overshoots.** It lands
  around sixteenth among commons, and the gap is instructive: in a deck built to blight it is the
  Flame Slash he describes, and in the average deck the additional cost is a real constraint. Take it
  high in a build that can pay; don't take it over the Eclipsed uncommon.
- **Thoughtweft Imbuer is the real "win a game below rare" card**, and it does not need a kithkin
  deck — changelings and a couple of one-drops are enough.
- **Moon-Vigil Adherents overperforms in both Elves and Kithkin.** It grows off every creature you
  control *and* every creature in your graveyard, and once large it dodges essentially all the red
  removal.
- **Bark of Doran does not hold up.** The high-toughness-creature theory is sound; the card is not.
- **The convoke support that doesn't earn a slot:** Sun-Dappled Celebrant, Evershrike's Gift,
  Goldmeadow Nomad, Stalactite Dagger. **Glister Bairn** has the build-around shape instead — poor on
  average, real when drawn in the right deck.
- **Auntie's Sentence is not being underrated.** The hunch that it might be a sleeper does not
  survive; it is exactly the mediocre top-deck it looks like.
- **Below the bar:** Brambleback Brute, Gutsplitter Gang. **Omni-Changeling** is fine rather than a
  breakout.

### Open questions the data can't settle

- **How much the "many A's, few B's" framing should change your picks.** It is a claim about the
  *shape* of the card pool rather than about any card, and a per-card win rate cannot test it
  directly. The archetype results are at least consistent with it: with the colours this close and
  the supported pairs this far ahead, what separates decks is unlikely to be raw colour choice, and
  impactful-card density is a plausible candidate.
- **Whether Red-Green is a real deck or a green-goodstuff accident.** Rough Drafts reports drafting it
  repeatedly and reaching it off green's depth. The data has it weak, on a small sample. Most likely
  both are true: it works for a strong drafter reading an open seat, and it is not a plan.
- **How many of your creature type you actually need — the sources disagree flatly.** Rough Drafts
  says the payoffs are low-commitment and five to eight of a type is usually enough, which is why the
  Eclipsed cycle is so good and why changelings keep you open. Limited Resources says count to 16–18
  and treat everything else as removal. Both are describing the same winning decks from different
  angles: LR's count includes the changelings and hybrids that Rough Drafts treats as "not really
  committing," so the gap is smaller than it reads. But the *pick-order* consequence is real and
  unresolved — LR would take a mediocre on-type creature over a good off-type one, and Rough Drafts
  would not. A per-card win rate cannot separate them, because both plans are inside the same
  archetype row.
- **Where Kithkin and Merfolk actually sit.** The three sources order the middle of the table
  differently: LR's on-air numbers put Kithkin second and Merfolk third; Lords of Limited has Merfolk
  second and Kithkin *last* of the five; the finished data has Merfolk, Kithkin and Elementals inside
  a fifteen-hundredths-of-a-point band. Nothing here is separable, and you should treat all three as
  the same deck-strength tier and let the seat decide. The top and bottom of the table — Elves first,
  Goblins fifth — is the part every source agrees on.
- **Which deck the field crowded into.** LR remember Merfolk as the most-drafted archetype of the
  format; the finished share column has Elves first at 23.8% with Merfolk second at 20.6%. Their read
  was formed mid-format and the field may genuinely have moved once the Elves result became known —
  which, if true, means the "contested lane" warning applies more to Elves late in a format's life
  than it did when the guides were recorded.
- **The format's own verdict.** Rough Drafts rates ECL highly and felt it rotated out early; Lords of
  Limited's retrospective is cooler on it; Limited Resources landed in between, with Marshall giving
  the set a flat C while LSV finished warmer on it than he started. Not a pick-order input, but worth
  knowing which lens each guide note on a tile was written through.
