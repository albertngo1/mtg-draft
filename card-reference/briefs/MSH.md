## Format brief — everything that isn't a single card

> MSH win rates run high across the board, so an average-looking number is genuinely average — read
> the gaps between cards, not the absolute figures. Four of the five sources recorded before or
> during release week: treat their letter grades as theory and the win rate beside them as the
> result. Samp's retrospective is the exception, recorded post-Pro-Tour with the data in hand.

### What the data says

*(The full archetype table renders directly above this brief.)*

**Azorius is the best deck and the most-drafted deck at once** — over a quarter of the field is in it
and it still clears second place comfortably. That combination is the strongest signal an archetype
can give: a contested lane that wins anyway is genuinely the best, not an unexploited niche.
Mono-White and Mono-Blue post the two highest win rates on the board outright, on small samples — the
same two colours, undiluted.

**Colour order: blue and white dead even at the top, then green, then black, and red a long way
behind.** The gap to red is enormous. The top six commons in the set are all white or blue — Hero in
Training, Trickster's Stratagem, Murdock's Crusade, S.H.I.E.L.D. Deployment Drone, Web Up, We Say
Thee Nay!. That is the whole colour story.

**Two seats are worth knowing about.** **Simic is the second-best archetype and the least-drafted** —
the field talked itself out of it, which makes it the best place to end up when white-blue is
contested. **Dimir is the mirror image**: drafted as often as Orzhov, finishing near the bottom. If
you are being passed good blue, the black half is what will cost you.

### The draft plan

1. **Be white-blue unless the seat says otherwise; be Simic when it does.** The two best colours,
   the best archetype, and a genuinely open second-best.
2. **Take cheap interaction over almost anything.** Removal is scarce, and this is the one thing all
   four guides said independently before the data existed — Limited Level-Ups called it at a premium
   more than usual, and Numot blamed both his losing drafts on being removal-light. The benchmarks:
   **Dark Deed**, **Punishing Punch**, **Web Up**, **Cruel Alliance**. **This is the one genuinely
   contested instruction in the brief** — the end-of-format source reversed it into value-creatures-
   first. See Open questions before you pass a Hero in Training for a kill spell.
3. **Ignore the signpost cycle; take the better card.** Of the twenty gold-and-hybrid signposts, six
   actively lose games, and only **Killmonger, Scourge of Wakanda** is worth a genuinely high pick.
   Several archetypes are good *despite* their signposts rather than because of them — Selesnya and
   Boros both post good numbers while both of their signposts are liabilities.
4. **Lightly-themed goodstuff midrange is a fine default.** No pair is dead, the fixing is deep, and
   every hard-committed synergy lane underperformed its billing. Limited Level-Ups' post-draft
   summary — the theme is good cards, and it is a midrange deck — is the read that survived contact
   with the numbers. **Every half-supported theme is a trap you should not pay pick-order for:**
   artifacts, equipment, self-targeting, heroes and villains. Take them when they fall into your lap;
   never concede card quality to reach one. The retrospective is blunt about the cause — the artifact
   theme exists because Iron Man exists, not because any mechanic pushes you into it.
5. **The best card in the set is The Super Hero Civil War**, by a distance. Take the bomb; the rest of
   the plan is flexible.

### What the numbers can't tell you

This is where the guides earn their place — none of it is visible in a win-rate column.

- **Four toughness is the magic number.** You need to attack through it and to kill it, which is why
  deal-3 effects underperform their rate. Build your curve around clearing or surviving that line.
- **No board wipes exist** — only deal-2-or-3-to-everything. Go-wide boards and sticky single threats
  are unusually safe, and a bomb that resolves with no sweeper in the format simply wins.
- **Bait removal with your mid-curve.** A plain four-mana 4/4 plays *better* than its grade in a slow
  format, because eating their removal on a medium threat is what protects your actual bomb. The
  exception holds for top-end fatties with no enter-the-battlefield value: those still get raced.
- **Don't straddle two synergy lanes.** Numot's clearest self-diagnosed error was a deck drifting
  between +1/+1 counters and artifacts and committing to neither. Counters live in Simic, artifacts
  in Izzet; they do not merge.
- **Splash for removal and a bomb, never for a pile of fatties.** Fixing is deep — gain-lands in about
  half of packs, an untapped rare dual cycle, land-cyclers, **Ant-Man's Army** and **Surveillance
  Room**. Numot demonstrated the failure mode himself: four- and five-colour piles that were
  bomb-heavy and interaction-light, losing to clean curves.
- **Whoever runs out of things to do loses.** The games run long, so top-end and card advantage carry
  more than the curve suggests. A treasure you make every turn and never spend is not advantage, and
  neither is ramp mana with nothing to cast off it.
- **Small tokens do not matter on a clogged board.** This is the cleanest explanation anyone offers
  for black underperforming: its go-wide payoffs and 2/1 menace bodies get blanked by exactly the
  1/3 walls and stalled ground that make flying good.
- **Don't draft red the way red's commons ask you to.** Aggressive red is a large part of why the
  colour's numbers look as bad as they do. The red deck that actually wins is the opposite: grindy,
  treasure-fuelled, splashing red's strong gold rares, paired with white removal or blue's
  counterspell, winning on inevitability rather than on damage.
- **"Nephews" — four- and five-mana creatures with No ETB, no Protection, no Haste — die to a
  two-mana removal spell for a game-losing tempo swing.** Nearly every signpost uncommon in the set
  is one. You will end up playing a couple; never make them the plan.

### Deckbuilding doctrine

- **The two-drop slot is scarce; the four-drop slot clogs.** Do not play bad filler two-drops — you
  0-for-1 yourself — but do count land-cyclers as two-drops (cycle on two, play a three on three).
  Typical shape: **3–5 real two-drops plus 2–3 cyclers, 16–17 lands, 17–18 creatures**, because so
  many of the engines are bodies.
- **Missing your third land drop is close to an auto-loss** in the grindy configurations, which is
  the argument for the cyclers over a marginal spell.
- **The basic land cyclers are the backbone of the format's splashing, not filler.** Their cycling
  cost is colourless and they fetch any basic — a real step up on the colour-gated versions from
  other sets — and they are the mechanism that lets a heavy base colour cast off-colour bombs. The
  release-week read that they were worse than they appear did not survive the format.
- **Splash mana-base rules:** go genuinely **heavy** in one base colour (9–10 sources), then add
  **one basic per splash colour**, two if there is a double pip. The recurring errors are playing
  more than three gain lands, double-splashing off a base colour that isn't heavy, and trimming
  lands to make room for cyclers.

### Where the field misprices cards

These are the gaps between what a card is worth and where it actually goes — the cheapest equity in
the format.

- **Wheels, and shouldn't:** **Rapid Rescue** is the single biggest gap in the set, then Giant-Sized
  Flying Ant, S.H.I.E.L.D. Helicarrier, HYDRA Infiltration, Depower and Super Suit.
- **Taken early, and shouldn't be:** Thunderbolts Conspiracy, War Machine, Spider-Man To the Rescue,
  Alien Invasion, Construct a Cosmic Cube, The Sentry, Shang-Chi and Black Panther Vanguard.
- **The worst uncommons in the set are signposts:** Beast, Madame Hydra, Mockingbird and Speedball.
  Being a gold uncommon is not a reason to take one.
- **Red is the worst colour *and* the most open lane, at the same time.** Both halves hold. The colour
  grade is correct — red's commons are genuinely bad — but the field read the same numbers and left,
  so everything after the first few picks arrives late. A guest on Rough Drafts reported eleven
  base-red decks across thirty drafts with seven trophies, drafting the grindy treasure build rather
  than the aggressive one the commons advertise. That is an archetype-level mispricing, which is
  exactly the kind a card win-rate column cannot show you.
- **HYDRA Troopers is not a reason to be in Golgari.**
- **Political Triumph is a top-ten uncommon while Construct a Cosmic Cube is a genuine bust** — the
  Plans are not one category. Value the early chapters; you rarely reach the last one.
- **HULK SMASH! is a real card.**
- **The bonus-sheet reprints carry no expert notes at all**, because set reviews skip known
  quantities — Sword of Fire and Ice, Path to Exile, Counterspell, Massacre Girl, Extinction Event.
  Their tiles show numbers and an AI take only. That is a coverage gap in the guides, not a signal
  that the cards are unimportant.

### Open questions the data can't settle

- **Removal first, or value creatures first?** The four release-week guides converge on cheap
  interaction at a premium, and the count backs them — roughly nineteen playable removal spells
  below rare. The end-of-format source started in that same place and then reversed it outright: the
  colour with the value creature beats the colour with the kill spell, because killing a creature
  that already drew a card is a two-for-one against *you*, and what actually decides games is the
  opponent resolving Hero in Training and S.H.I.E.L.D. Deployment Drone. Both halves survive the
  data — removal really is scarce, and the two best colours really are the ones holding the value
  creatures rather than the removal. The practical resolution: scarcity is a reason to take the
  removal you are passed, not a reason to pass a better creature reaching for it.
- **Was the set well designed, and does that matter to a drafter?** The retrospective verdict is
  that MSH is "a little bit of a mess" — nearly every creature above common being a legend crowded
  out mechanical design space, so nothing pushes you into any of the half-themes. The same source
  still rates the *gameplay* highly. This is not an idle aesthetic complaint: it is the reason "take
  the best card" beats every synergy plan here, and the reason the signpost cycle is safe to ignore.
- **How fast is this format, really?** Limited Level-Ups and Lords of Limited converge on slow and
  grindy — board stalls, flying breaks them, no sweepers, missing land drop three loses. Numot, from
  the other side of the table, insists the loudest lesson of his drafts was that playing first and
  curving out is overpowered, and that he lost repeatedly to clean 2→3→4 curves before any stall
  formed. Card-level win rates cannot adjudicate this. Both are true in practice: the format grinds
  when neither player is ahead, and punishes you hard when you are the one durdling. Build a deck
  that can do both, and take the play.
