## Format brief — everything that isn't a single card

> **Read the win-rate column carefully here.** SOS is a soup format: a top archetype is a greedy
> multicolour pile, and 17Lands splits that deck's record across dozens of invisible three- and
> four-colour builds while showing clean two-colour decks in full. The one measurable trace is that
> the **colorless cards post the highest win rate of any group in the set** — above every actual
> colour — because the soup staples are colorless. A proxy, not proof.
>
> This is also the tightest colour spread in the reference. Colour differences are near noise, and an
> aggregate win rate here is a story about the metagame rather than a power ranking — several cards
> with poor aggregate numbers have real ceilings in the one deck built to want them.

### What the data says

*(The full archetype table renders directly above this brief.)*

**The two white colleges are a tier of their own** — the gap from second to third is enormous for a
five-archetype format. Limited Resources' settled advice, that if you only care about winning and the
table is open you should be in white-black or white-red, is the plain right answer.

**The format is polarized, and the middle is what loses.** Two poles: blue over-the-top control (UR,
and the multicolour piles that grow out of it) at one end; white aggro — Lorehold and Silverquill — at
the other, existing to punish it. Anything trying to sit between them is squeezed from both
directions, which is the cleanest explanation of why the two white colleges are a tier apart and the
grindy midrange college is not. Ask which pole you are on every draft, and never build a white deck
that is "kind of midrange".

**Prismari is the trap.** Last of the five *and* the second most-drafted — nearly a quarter of the
field in the worst college. If you want a two-colour deck, this is the one to leave.

**But look at who else is down there.** Prismari and Witherbloom finish **0.05pp apart** — that is a
tie being read as a ranking. Both new sources name **Witherbloom**, not Prismari, as the college the
field misdrafts, and one of them points out that the three highest-picked gold commons in the whole
set sit in it. See the open question at the end; the actionable version is the same either way, which
is that the white colleges are underfull and two of the other three are overfull.

**Both new sources call Lorehold the best deck, and both were surprised by it.** The prerelease
consensus had Lorehold as the *worst* college and Witherbloom as a good one; the format inverted both
exactly. The table still has Silverquill 0.39pp ahead, so treat this as a tie broken by play
experience rather than by the numbers — and note that Lorehold carries the larger metagame share
while *staying* open, because the field keeps spending its early picks on the black and green
commons.

**Colour order: white, then red, blue, black and green, all bunched.** No colour is unplayable and
none is a lock.

**The soup payoffs, and note how late two of them go:** Together as One is the best card in the set,
then Mathemagics, **Snarl Song** (the best uncommon), **Arcane Omens** (which still wheels),
Exhibition Tidecaller, Sundering Archaic, Divergent Equation and Wisdom of Ages.

**Potioner's Trove is the keystone and the field still passes it** — a top-seven common routinely
available at pick five.

**Best commons:** Elite Interceptor and Pursue the Past (which wheels) are effectively tied for
first, then Rubble Rouser, Unsubtle Mockery, Last Gasp, Cost of Brilliance, Render Speechless and
Potioner's Trove.

### The draft plan

1. **Default to a white college — Silverquill or Lorehold — and let pack one pick the other half.**
   Open a strong white card and see whether black or red is flowing. The two are close enough that
   you should simply take the open one. Once you have started white, put the blinders on — there are
   enough good white cards to go around that a white start ends in a good white deck most of the
   time. If the white lane looks contested around picks 4–5, **lean specifically into red**: red's
   best cards are equally good in Lorehold aggro and in blue-red control, so red is the pick that
   keeps both doors open.
2. **Never force soup. It needs a payoff first.** All five sources say this independently, and it is
   the most-agreed claim across them — the two newest phrase it as *draft it when the fixing is
   flowing, never by intent*, and both warn that taking the Converge payoffs early is its own trap.
   Commit only once you have at least one over-the-top payoff, and run the self-check as a single
   question: am I seeing enough fixing to reach four colours reliably? "Bad soup" — fixing with no
   payoff, or payoffs with no early interaction — loses to everything.
3. **Take Potioner's Trove and Arcane Omens above where the field takes them.** Both are top-tier and
   both wheel.
4. **Be on the play, and expect long games.** Games run long enough that decking is a real win
   condition and life gain is everywhere, but spending mana every turn still beats the extra card —
   especially with a five-mana hand-emptier legal in every deck.
5. **Prefer the card that does two things.** The reason a soup deck out-cards a grindy deck is that
   its spells are removal *and* draw at once: Stress Dream, Traumatic Critique, Stock Up.
6. **Your actual job is finding the one open college.** SOS is an all-gold, no-hybrid guild set —
   every college payoff demands both colours — so being alone in a lane pays far more here than in a
   set with hybrid or splashable payoffs: the gold density then hands you the maximum-quality version
   of that deck automatically, draft after draft. One host opened the format arguing that the
   colleges are not monolithic and that you should chase the power in front of you, and reversed
   himself by the retrospective for exactly this reason. So read signals at picks 4–5 and again on
   the wheel at pick 9, with a blunt rubric: **zero playables back from a pack = closed, one =
   contested, two or three = wide open.** Identify a two-colour base by the end of pack one, lock it
   by the middle of pack two, and only then decide how many extra colours (possibly zero) to add.

### What the numbers can't tell you

- **Don't play around specific answers; do sequence around counter mana.** There are too many possible
  instants per colour to play around any one — make the straightforward play, but split your turn so
  a counterspell only stops half of it.
- **Sandbag your prepared-spell finishers.** The soup decks have to tap low to deploy their payoffs;
  that is the window.
- **Time the mill finisher for a tapped-out opponent, and count their mana first.** Decking is a real
  plan here, not a novelty.
- **Don't run fragile creatures in a spell deck.** Removal is cheap, plentiful and good in every
  colour and rarity — a two-toughness body that isn't doing anything dies for free.
- **Avoid two-toughness creatures that can't grow, especially at three-plus mana.** They fold to the
  cheap red removal and let the slow decks do their thing. The mirror of that rule is the one to build
  toward: **four toughness blocks every unmodified two-drop in the format and dodges the premium cheap
  black removal**, so a four-toughness body up the curve trades at a mana advantage.
- **Don't fall out of your role.** Aggro must be ahead; soup needs parity; being behind in either is
  where games are lost fast.
- **Don't be precious with prepared creatures — being precious is the format's most common play
  mistake.** Trade one off for nothing when the board demands it; securing parity and keeping your
  life total high is what buys you the option to hold a valuable prepared creature later. The same
  rule from the other side: **kill a prepared creature with its "becomes prepared" trigger still on
  the stack**, so they never get the spell at all.
- **Decide early whether the game is about value or tempo.** Named as the single biggest coaching
  note of the format, and it is genuinely hard to call. The power level is high enough that the
  mistake snowballs — miss one beat pressuring a blue deck and it simply draws past you.
- **Expect your rares to die, and bank a trigger before you commit one.** Removal is cheap and
  everywhere, so hold value permanents until playing one buys you at least one trigger rather than
  running them out on curve.
- **Quandrix is interaction-starved, not power-starved.** Numot's refinement is the useful one: don't
  force GU tempo — base it blue-red and splash green, or accept you have no removal beyond counters.
  One dissent worth logging rather than merging: a guest on the newest source demonstrated a real
  blue-green tempo deck built on counterspells, protection and flash rather than on threats, and the
  host — who had publicly said it wasn't possible — conceded after trophying with it himself. Treat it
  as narrow and matchup-polarised (it eats the multicolour decks, it loses badly to Silverquill), and
  as something to consider around halfway through pack one, never a pick-one commitment.

### Deckbuilding doctrine

- **17 lands standard. 18 in a shaky soup base or with rummage/pitch outlets. 16 only for tight
  low-curve aggro.**
- **Splash benchmark: roughly 7–8 sources per main colour**, and a single-basic splash is justified by
  about five Converge cards. Two colours wants 2–3 on-colour tapped duals; multicolour wants 4–6.
  Two tapped duals is the Goldilocks number even in a clean two-colour deck, because the set is dense
  with **gold two-drops** — you need both colours available on turn two.
- **The fixing-when-poor heuristic runs backwards from instinct:** if your mana is already fine, take
  power; the off-colour fixer pays off most exactly when your mana is bad.
- **Great Hall of the Biblioplex beats Terramorphic Expanse for Converge.** Terramorphic yields one
  colour and eats a basic; the true five-colour land keeps every colour live.
- **Silverquill needs its own targets:** six-plus two-drop creatures and twelve-plus creatures total,
  or your targeted spells have nothing to point at.
- **Card filtering beats raw card advantage**, because hitting land drops matters enormously and there
  is always somewhere to sink mana.
- **Play more top end than feels right: about two seven-drops, three sixes and plenty of fives** in a
  slow deck. Ramp and rocks make a seven play like a six, and the slow decks were roughly half the
  format rather than the one draft in ten they were predicted to be.
- **Removal counts by deck: 5–6 in a white aggro deck, 10–11 in a control deck**, 6–8 in the black
  small-game builds. Removal never goes dead here because there is so much card draw to buy, and card
  draw always has something to buy — that pairing is the format's core engine. Counterspells count as
  interaction, and they are genuinely good, because everyone taps out for five- to seven-mana spells.
- **Repartee wants 8–9 ways to target your own creatures**, counting removal and tricks, not just
  dedicated pump. It is the set's one real deckbuilding density puzzle; if you reach pack three short,
  the one-mana tricks wheel every time.

### Where the field misprices cards

- **The field's two big mistakes were systematic, not scattered.** One host re-graded his own set
  review on air after a month of play and found a pattern rather than a list. He had under-rated
  (a) **everything supporting the Converge deck — the payoffs *and* the fixing that enables them** —
  and (b) **every over-the-top expensive spell.** Both misses share one cause: the slow decks were
  assumed to show up about one draft in ten, and they were closer to *half the format*. If a card is
  in either bucket, move it up. The second-order version of the same error, reached independently by
  the other new source: under-rating **Potioner's Trove** dragged every Converge payoff down with it,
  because the payoffs were being graded on how often you would actually get there.
- **The field misprices the colleges, not only the cards.** The prerelease consensus called Lorehold
  the worst college and liked Witherbloom; the format inverted both, and the correction never fully
  propagated — the black and green commons stayed at the top of the field's early picks all format,
  which is precisely why Lorehold kept flowing. Two to two-and-a-half Lorehold drafters per table is
  supportable.
- **Flashback is worth more than it reads — this is a rule, not a card fact.** Both new sources
  arrived at it separately: a removal spell with flashback beats the same spell without it by more
  than the text suggests, even when the flashback cost is clunky, and even an eight-mana flashback
  gets paid more often than you would guess in a format this mana-rich.
- **Wheels, and shouldn't:** **Arcane Omens** by far the biggest gap in the set, then Pursue the Past,
  Cost of Brilliance, Run Behind, Archaic's Agony, Embrace the Paradox, Quick Study, Masterful
  Flourish and Essence Scatter. Counterspells as a class go too late here: in a format where
  everybody taps out for five-, six- and seven-mana spells, the field still reads them as filler.
- **Taken early, and shouldn't be:** Comforting Counsel worst of all, then Armageddon, Leech
  Collector, Nita, **Forum Necroscribe**, Abigale, Joined Researchers, Summoned Dromedary and
  **Eternal Student**.
- **The whole UR aggro creature suite is below baseline**, which is why Prismari finishes last: Deluge
  Virtuoso, Spectacular Skywhale, Muse Seeker, **Tackle Artist**, Elemental Mascot and **Expressive
  Firedancer**. The last two are the ones Numot said to take every copy of.
- **Daydream is the worst card in the set relative to its reputation.**
- **Diary of Dreams and Root Manipulation are both below-average uncommons** despite being pitched as
  engines.
- **Render Speechless is available, not impactful.** You can always have one; having it changes little.
  Imperious Inkmage and Killian's Confidence share the shape: Silverquill's commons make the deck
  without winning the game you drew them in.
- **Rubble Rouser is the Lorehold engine; Garrison Excavator is the part you can miss.**
- **Grave Researcher is a rare you're first-picking for a below-average result.**
- **The bonus sheet is bimodal — check the tile.** One card per pack, ranging from Emeritus of
  Ideation at the top to Archmage Emeritus near the bottom. Several of those tiles rest on a small
  fraction of the games a common's does, so their numbers carry far wider error bars. Treat any
  *prerelease* grade on a bonus-sheet card as provisional as well: the one host who ranked the sheet
  twice moved a great deal of it hard in both directions once he had a month of reps, and his April
  bonus-sheet episode is the single place in his run where even the attribution of who said what is
  unreliable. Prefer the later re-rank; where only the April grade exists, don't lean on it.

### Open questions the data can't settle

- **How good is soup, really?** The three original sources call it the best thing to be doing and all
  three argue 17Lands structurally cannot see it. They are describing a real limitation, not excusing a bad
  take — but note how tight the colour spread is, so even a large correction doesn't move much. The
  practical synthesis: soup is a real ceiling that needs payoffs first; the white colleges are the
  reliable floor. The two new sources sharpen the question rather than settling it. One of them
  refuses the word "soup" altogether and splits the pile into **base-green midrange** and **base-blue
  control**, which is a useful discipline: this set does *not* reward the classic plan of hoarding
  fixing to cash in late rares, because its best rares want a dedicated deck rather than a splash.
  The other ends the format saying the multicolour deck was simply too easy to draft and outshone the
  five colleges. So the live disagreement is about how to *build* it, not whether it is real.
- **"Don't pick commons picks 1–8."** One Lords of Limited host holds this hard; the other explicitly
  won't co-sign it. The data can't settle a draft-strategy claim, but note that the format's best
  commons — Elite Interceptor, Pursue the Past, Potioner's Trove — beat a great many uncommons, so
  the hard version of the rule costs real equity.
- **Prismari or Witherbloom — which is the field's real trap?** The two rows finish 0.05pp apart, so
  the table genuinely cannot separate them. The three original sources point at Prismari: a quarter of
  the field in the college whose aggressive creature suite is below baseline. Both new sources point
  at Witherbloom instead — the three highest-picked gold commons in the set are all in it, and one
  host says you could have ignored the whole college for the entire format and kept a good win rate.
  Both can be true at once, and neither claim is settled by the numbers. What is settled: don't be
  the second person at your table in either of them.
- **Was Witherbloom bad, or only over-drafted?** The two new sources split on this and neither is
  hedging. One ends the format walking his own criticism back — the power level was there, the problem
  was purely the Arena metagame, and he cites tournament tables where the top bracket was Witherbloom
  mirrors; he would draft it happily at a field that evaluates correctly. The other calls it flatly
  the worst college and says it stayed ignorable to the end. The difference is which population you
  are drafting against, and nothing in the archetype table distinguishes the two.
- **Lorehold versus Silverquill.** Narrowed, not settled. The table has Silverquill ahead by 0.39pp
  and Lorehold holds the larger metagame share; both new sources, having drafted the format to its
  end, call Lorehold the best deck outright and one names it the surprise of the format. Weigh that
  against a 0.39pp gap and the advice stands: take the open one — but if they look equally open, the
  play experience points at Lorehold.
