## Format brief — everything that isn't a single card

> **No common or uncommon in FIN carries a Limited Resources letter grade** — only the Rare & Mythic
> review was captured, so an absent grade on a common tile is a missing source, not a low opinion.
> FIN otherwise has the largest sample in this reference and its common/uncommon numbers are settled;
> the bonus-sheet reprints are the exception and deserve much wider error bars.

### What the data says

*(The full archetype table renders directly above this brief.)*

**FIN is the most balanced set Limited Resources have ever discussed, and that is the single most
important fact about drafting it.** Their end-of-format read: the archetypes ranked **#9 through #1
sit inside a 2.2% total win-rate band**, with only Gruul about another 2% adrift below them. Both
hosts graded the set an A on that basis. The practical consequence is that **archetype choice is
nearly free and lane-reading is nearly everything** — you can draft any of nine pairs and win with
it, so the skill being tested is reading which one is open, not knowing which one is best. The
stretch goal they set is to know all ten pairs well enough to take whichever one the table hands you.
Read the archetype table above as a ranking with almost no gaps in it rather than a hierarchy.

**Izzet is the best deck and the most-drafted deck at once.** A contested lane that wins anyway is
the strongest signal an archetype can give. The mechanical reason is real: **tiered spells** give UR
cheap functional early plays that later become expensive payoffs, so the deck's early game and late
game are the same cards.

**And UR is the format's flagship miss.** LR heard the design brief — a blue-red deck that pays you
for casting four-mana noncreature spells — and doubted it out loud: "that just sounds like that's not
going to work." It became the best-performing archetype in the set on both play rate and win rate,
and by the retrospective they named its key common "underrated by just about everyone." The reason
the deck works is the reason it sounded bad: the payoffs key off **mana actually spent, not printed
cost**, so the tiered spells you already wanted to play count on their own. You are not distorting a
deck to hit a condition; you are nudging a normal deck a little further in a direction it was already
going.

**Colour order: blue first, then white and black, green behind them, red last.** Rough Drafts got to
blue by the right method — reading colour strength off the *archetype* table rather than off card
evaluations, and noticing that all four blue pairs clear the bar. Note that the Lords of Limited
retrospective called green the worst colour and white fourth; the finished data says **red is weakest
and white is second**.

**Two seats worth knowing.** **Boros is the most under-drafted good deck in the format** — second
best, a small slice of the field, and rated merely B− by the guides. **Dimir is the mirror image**:
heavily drafted for a bottom-half finish, despite being called the format's #2 pair pre-data. If you
want a contrarian seat, it is equipment aggro, not the grindy blue-black pile.

**Rakdos and Gruul are the two to avoid.** The guides agree on Gruul; on Rakdos they believed the data
under-rated it, and it does not. Limited Resources make Gruul the one lane in the format they would
actively tell you to bail on, and supply the cleanest evidence for it: **both Pro Tour feature
drafters forced Gruul anyway and both regretted it on record.** Their Rakdos read is softer than the
"avoid" framing — LSV drafted it least of any pair and says so, which is a reason to discount his
Rakdos card evaluations rather than a defence of the archetype.

**The best commons:** **White Auracite** leads, then Combat Tutorial, Town Greeter, Dragoon's Wyvern,
**Sephiroth's Intervention** (the best removal common and a genuine first pick), Thunder Magic,
Cornered by Black Mages, Ice Magic and Sahagin.

**The best uncommons:** **Dragoon's Lance**, **Summon: Fenrir**, **Choco-Comet** (the best red card
below rare), then Cloud of Darkness, Samurai's Katana, Delivery Moogle and Sazh's Chocobo.

**The rares decide games.** Atraxa, Grand Unifier has the single largest draw-impact number anywhere
in this reference; Dion, Ardyn, Nibelheim Aflame, Sazh Katzroy and Urza are the rest of the top tier.

### The draft plan

1. **Start blue, and let the seat pick the second colour.** Best colour, best archetype, and all four
   of its pairs clear the field average.
2. **Pack 4–6 pieces of interaction.** This is the format's biggest documented correction — the
   experts went from "two or three is enough" before playing to loading up once the games were in,
   and nothing in the data argues. Removal that answers a four-toughness body is worth more than its
   rate.
3. **Prioritise evasion.** The ground clogs with tokens, hero tokens and 3/3s; flying is what actually
   ends games.
4. **Take the mono-coloured interaction over the gold signpost, all else equal.** A removal spell fits
   more decks than a two-colour card does, and in a format that rewards lane-finding that flexibility
   is worth a small power concession early.
5. **Don't splash.** Rough Drafts' settled position is that FIN barely wants it: the pairs are
   self-sufficient, and an off-colour card adds less here than in a goodstuff format.

### What the numbers can't tell you

- **Four toughness is the magic number.** The format's cheap removal tops out at 3 damage at base
  rate, so a four-toughness body carries a real layer of protection. This is why blue's cheap commons
  punch above their stat line, and why your own deal-3 effects underperform their reputation.
- **Kill the 1/1 with the equipment attached.** Job Select creates a hero token that auto-equips.
  Every instinct says to ignore the 1/1 and save removal for a real threat; the correct play is
  usually to kill the token while the equipment is on it. The guides flag that this stays
  counterintuitive even after you understand it.
- **The critical turn is later than it used to be.** Games are not longer, but the decisive combat has
  drifted from around turn four or five to **turn six or seven** — formats are less snowbally because
  cheap creatures were powered down while cheap interaction got better. You are punished less for a
  slow start than instinct suggests, and rewarded more for holding up an answer.
- **Don't legend-rule yourself.** Every signpost uncommon is a legend — check the battlefield before
  you flash one back.
- **Card filtering is a role, and only some decks need it filled.** UR and BR already get theirs from
  the non-creature spells they want to cast; RW and WG often have no other source. Judge the filtering
  cards by whether the seat you're in still needs the role.
- **Green has to be pushed on you.** One early green card is not a commitment — it takes multiple
  premium green cards *plus* an open pair.
- **Games resolve into attrition, not tempo — this is the format's central gameplay fact.** LSV's
  settled read: "these games didn't come down to tempo fights as often as I found. We both kind of
  answered each other's stuff, and whoever can accumulate more won." **Prioritise cards that
  accumulate** — flashback, recursion, self-mill that becomes a resource, card draw with a body
  attached. It is why several slow value cards beat their reviews and why a card that trades once and
  is gone is worth less here than its rate says. It also reframes rule 2 above: you want interaction
  because it is *cheap and plentiful*, not because you are fighting a tempo war.
- **This is a Pauper set, and common removal is why.** Every colour has real removal at common —
  White Auracite and Slash of Light in white; Ice Magic, Syncopate, Stuck in Summoner's Sanctum and
  Swallowed by Leviathan in blue; Sephiroth's Intervention, Vayne's Treachery and Cornered by Black
  Mages in black; Thunder Magic, Suplex, Light of Judgment and Fire Magic in red. Both LR hosts:
  "you could have a lot of great decks without a single rare." Opening a bomb does not decide the
  game because the table is holding the answer, and *your* bomb is not a licence to build loosely.
- **Have cheap plays so you live to cast your expensive ones.** The format is not slow, but it isn't
  aggression-driven either — you feel it badly when you're not on the board, and you also never run
  out of gas. That combination makes midrange fully viable: LR's best deck of the format had four
  seven-drops and an eight-drop, held up by real early plays. Curve low so you can afford to curve
  high.
- **Saga creatures: take the hits from three-chapter ones, kill the four-chapter ones.** A clean
  combat heuristic that no win rate encodes. Four chapters means eight-plus damage of accumulated
  value, and eating that while doing something else is not a plan.
- **Sealed is good here and it is a different format.** It plays slower and more controlling by
  default, big three-colour fixing is the norm, blue is the most-played colour, and **white and red
  are the two weakest** because their aggressive themes don't translate. Do not carry your draft
  colour order into a sealed pool.
- **Signals:** late removal wheeling is a massive open-lane tell, because good drafters don't pass it.
  Three consecutive packs with no white means your right-hand neighbour is heavy white — pivot.
  Seeing the same signpost twice late is a strong confirmation to commit.

### Deckbuilding doctrine

- **Decide whether you are building for power or for synergy.** Some FIN decks want half a dozen
  genuinely powerful cards and a normal Magic shell around them; others want the pair's synergy. The
  failure mode is a deck that is neither.
- **Saga creatures are context cards, not centrepieces** — with two exceptions worth building toward,
  **Summon: Fenrir** and **Summon: Fat Chocobo**.
- **Don't pick Town lands early.** The Towns payoffs work when you assemble them organically; the
  affinity-for-Towns draw spell is a genuine gem only once you already have a handful.
- **Blitz Ball is a fine artifact, not a five-colour enabler** — it ramps, triggers artifact
  synergies, and cashes in. It does not license a legends pile.
- **Don't build the UR deck as ten four-mana spells.** The shape is roughly five genuine four-drops
  plus five cheap flexible cards you can *choose* to overpay on. Loading up on actual four-drops
  makes it clunky and defeats the mechanic. Same rule from the other side: the noncreature spells
  that leave bodies behind *are* your creatures — don't then also fill the deck with mediocre real
  ones.
- **The synergy dial in this format sits around halfway.** Real theme decks exist (UR four-cost, UW
  artifacts, BG self-mill, RB wizards) and so do decks that are simply a pile of good cards, and both
  win. That is the concrete form of the power-versus-synergy question below: the format supports
  either answer, which is exactly why picking one and building to it beats drifting between them.
- **The self-mill deck wants every card to be a permanent.** Perfectly good instants and sorceries
  are actively bad in BG because the mill needs to hit bodies and the payoffs count permanents. A
  card being good is not the test; a card being *countable* is.

### Where the field misprices cards

- **Taken early, and shouldn't be.** **Aettir and Priwen** and **The Masamune** are the two worst early
  picks in the set — both legendary artifacts, both taken in the first handful of picks, both actively
  losing games. Then Vaan, The Earth Crystal, Stroke of Midnight, Ishgard, Genji Glove, Jumbo Cactuar,
  Stiltzkin and The Wind Crystal. **A card being a legendary artifact or a Crystal is not a reason to
  take it.**
- **Wheels, and shouldn't.** **Sorceress's Schemes** is Rough Drafts' find and the clearest bargain in
  the set — a narrow graveyard-recursion card that posts a strong number anyway, best in UR and BR.
  Then **Syncopate**, Blazing Bomb, Shinra Reinforcements, Rufus Shinra, **PuPu UFO**, Malboro,
  Scorpion Sentinel and **Magic Damper**, which comes back latest of all.
- **Magitek Infantry is the clearest case of a card good decks run that doesn't win you games.** Play
  it; don't take it early.
- **Towns are under-taken, and this cuts against the doctrine above.** Limited Resources' settled
  position is that even a plain two-colour deck wants about two towns, because "you're just going to
  get mana screwed maybe one less game over the course of the draft — that's huge," and the
  towns/ramp decks want five to seven. The doctrine bullet above is about not spending *early picks*
  on them, which is a different claim, but the two do collide on how highly to take the third or
  fourth one. LR add the caveat that keeps this honest: **the town payoffs are thin.** Almost
  everything that looks like a towns reward is really paying you for three colours or for big mana,
  not for towns as a card type.
- **Summon: G.F. Ifrit is genuinely weak**, and Rough Drafts agrees while adding the useful nuance:
  it is worst in UR and BR, which already have their filtering, and playable in RW where nothing else
  fills that role.
- **Reach the Horizon and Black Mage's Rod are both fine-to-mediocre.** The episodes built around them
  are about splash discipline and equipment-as-payoff, not about the cards.
- **The bonus sheet largely missed, with two exceptions:** Lightning Bolt and Fatal Push are the
  reprints that mattered. ⚠ Every bonus-sheet tile rests on a small fraction of the games a common
  does — Atraxa's spectacular numbers included. Read them with much wider error bars.

### Open questions the data can't settle

- **Whether Rakdos was really under-rated.** The guides, especially after playing it out, believed
  17Lands under-sold it and one host soft-forced it late in the format. The finished sample has it
  near the bottom. The honest reading is that a strong drafter reading an open seat can do well in a
  pair the field is right to avoid — which is a claim about the drafter, not the archetype.
- **Whether the rares decide games or the commons do.** This brief says the rares decide games, and
  the draw-impact numbers back it. Limited Resources say the opposite as a *format* claim — "you
  could have a lot of great decks without a single rare," because common removal in every colour
  answers the bombs. Both can be true at once and probably are: a resolved bomb still wins the game
  it resolves in, and the format still has enough answers that you cannot draft as though yours will.
  The pick-order consequence is small; the *deckbuilding* consequence is not. Build a deck that beats
  their bomb rather than one that assumes yours arrives.
- **How much three-colour is actually correct.** The draft plan says don't splash, on Rough Drafts'
  settled read that the pairs are self-sufficient. Limited Resources describe two real three-colour
  decks they won with — a Grixis control build and a Sultai towns pile, the latter taking LSV two
  trophies — and would let you splash a fourth colour for a genuinely great card. The reconciliation
  they offer themselves is the tightest constraint in either position: those decks work **only when
  the interaction is cheap.** Tap land into tap land into a two-mana removal spell is fine; the same
  mana with expensive interaction is unplayable. If you cannot say which cheap removal spell your
  third colour is being built around, the "don't splash" rule is the one that applies to you.
- **How much the "power versus synergy" frame should change a pick.** It is a claim about deck
  archetypes rather than cards, and a per-card win rate can't test it. It is still the most useful
  deckbuilding question to ask yourself at the end of pack two.
- **What a FIN retrospective would have said.** Rough Drafts moved on before writing one, so its notes
  are all early-to-mid format. Where its reads and the finished data agree — blue on top, Sorceress's
  Schemes being real, Ifrit being weak — that agreement is worth more than usual, since it was reached
  without the benefit of the settled sample. **Limited Resources did write one, and it lands on two of
  those three independently:** its Sunset Show has Sorceress's Schemes as a card that beat its review
  and Ifrit as one to stop playing. Rough Drafts got there earlier and with less evidence, which is
  the stronger version of being right.
