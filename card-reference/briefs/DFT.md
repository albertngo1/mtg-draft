## Format brief — everything that isn't a single card

> DFT is full of big commons that live in good decks without winning the game you drew them in —
> when a card's win rate and its impact-when-drawn disagree here, believe the second. The Limited
> Level-Ups source names roughly fifty of 281 cards and grades none of them: authority on the
> *format*, silent on individual cards. Narrow source base overall; lean on the tiles.

### What the data says

*(The full archetype table renders directly above this brief.)*

**The three green pairs are the top three, and they are a statistical tie.** Golgari is also the
most-drafted archetype in the format, which makes it the safest default rather than a hidden gem;
**Selesnya is the underdrafted one** — the same tier, a fraction of the field.

**Boros is the worst archetype by a wide margin**, further below ninth place than any other gap in
the set. It is also the least-drafted, so the field has this right.

**Colour order: green, then black and blue close behind, then white and red a real step back.**
LLU's post-format read puts it **green > blue > black > red > white**, with green and blue as the two
strong colours. Green on top is agreed by everyone. **The disagreement is white:** LLU calls it the
worst colour in the set and "pretty bad," while the table above has it ahead of red. Both readings
survive if white's floor is low and its ceiling is narrow — see the white-deck construction rule in
the doctrine below.

**Blue's uncommons are the best in the format and its commons are not** — the sharpest observation in
either guide. Spikeshell Harrier, Stock Up, Rangers' Refueler and Transit Mage are four elite
uncommons sitting on top of a thoroughly ordinary common tier. That split is the whole colour story,
and a second source arrives at it independently — LLU's blue section is almost entirely uncommons,
and names those same four.

**Best commons:** Stampeding Scurryfoot is the best in the set, then Engine Rat, Wreckage Wickerfolk,
Pactdoll Terror, Grim Bauble, Migrating Ketradon, Run Over, **Ride's End** (the best white common and
the cleanest exile effect in the format) and Spin Out.

**Best uncommon: Thundering Broodwagon** — the Golgari signpost, and the strongest single piece of
evidence for the green pairs owning the format.

### The draft plan

1. **Be in green. Which green pair matters less than being in green at all** — the top three are
   indistinguishable.
2. **Take blue uncommons aggressively and blue commons late.** The gap between them is the largest
   colour-internal split in the set.
3. **Prefer three-power or three-toughness two-drops.** The format is slower than a vehicle set has
   any right to be, and vanilla 2/2s brick against the 1/3s and die to the cheap black removal — both
   of the two-drops the guide names as disappointments, **Leonin Surveyor** and **Gilded Ghoda**, are
   exactly that shape.
4. **Do something to the board on two, three and four.** LLU's core format rule, stated from full
   hindsight: the two-drops aren't what kill you, falling behind is — "if you're not affecting the
   board on turn two, on turn three, on turn four, things can snowball out of control very, very
   quickly." Passing turns to hold up interaction is how you lose games you were not losing. **Note
   this sits in tension with the slower read below** and with "answer the resolved threat rather than
   racing it"; the two are not reconciled, so see the open question at the end.
5. **Have an answer for the big green commons.** Not because they're broken — Hazard of the Dunes and
   Migrating Ketradon are good rather than scary — but because green is where the format's density
   lives, and **Ride's End** is the clean answer.
6. **Buy the exhaust and Start Your Engines payoffs late.** Both mechanics reward a slow format, but
   the specific payoffs get taken above their worth (see below). Let them come to you.

### What the numbers can't tell you

- **Raw power and toughness is the master evaluation lens in this set.** Stats do triple duty here:
  they crew vehicles, they saddle mounts, and they win the combat the format is actually built
  around. A plainly overstated body with no text is therefore worth more than the same card would be
  in almost any other set — which is the mechanism behind the "big commons" calibration at the top of
  this page, and the reason a vanilla-ish beater can be a top uncommon.
- **Crew cost is the whole evaluation on a vehicle.** Crew 1 is in a different class from crew 3, and
  the pilot tokens exist to paper over that gap. Read the crew number before the body.
- **Use exhaust as a mana sink, not a plan.** In a format this slow you reach the activation
  threshold regularly, which is exactly why the abilities are worth having and not worth building
  around. **Exhaust is also a reliable quality signal on sight** — LLU's read is that it is very rare
  to see an exhaust card that isn't good. Hold both: the ability almost always makes the card better,
  and the cards that pay you for *counting* exhausts still aren't worth an early pick.
- **The archetype themes are loose — you are not locked into your pair's mechanic.** A UR deck with
  no discard theme at all, running exhaust instead, is a normal and fine outcome. Take the better
  card and let the deck name itself; the signpost mechanic is a suggestion, not a contract.
- **Start Your Engines needs three or four turns to matter.** Treat max-speed text as a late-game
  bonus, and get there with the one-two punch: deploy a threat, then remove a blocker so the attack
  connects and ratchets speed. **The rule that makes the maths work:** you cannot gain more than one
  speed per turn — except on the turn you first switch the mechanic on, where casting the card and
  then connecting gets you to speed 2 immediately. Cheap evasive bodies, not big ones, are what
  actually carry you to max speed.
- **Sequence a cycler before your land drop** when you're digging — the extra card can change which
  land you keep, and cycling is how the discard decks find their payoff.
- **Answer the resolved threat rather than racing it.** Board stalls are largely a green-mirror
  phenomenon; against green, exile removal and a six-power blocker are the two things that buy the
  turn back.

### Deckbuilding doctrine

- **17 lands standard.** The Push the Limit shell is the exception: 15 with three or four cyclers, and
  14 only with ten-plus. Don't cut lands without the cyclers to justify it.
- **Three or four vehicles, and don't force them.** This is a vehicle set that is not *about*
  vehicles — there is little vehicle-matters payoff, and vehicles compete with each other for the
  creatures that crew them, so past three or four they stop working. Spend those slots on the good
  ones and sideboard the mediocre ones.
- **Any white deck should be heavy in the other colour and light on white.** LLU's construction rule
  is explicit: in GW you want heavy green and a small number of *selected* white cards, and the same
  shape applies to WB. White's playables run out fast; be there for the few excellent ones, not for
  the colour.
- **Play one colorless Start Your Engines land** if your mana base can absorb it; more than that is
  greed. **LLU disagrees and would play zero** — the general objection being that a colorless land
  carries a real cost and has to earn it, and these mostly don't. The disagreement is narrow: one
  land is the most either side is arguing about, so if your mana is at all tight, cut it.
- **Splash intentionally or not at all.** The fixing exists but needs real picks — this is not a
  format that hands it to you.

### Where the field misprices cards

- **Taken early, and shouldn't be:** Bloodghast, Spire Mechcycle, Pacesetter Paragon, Adrenaline
  Jockey, Cloudspire Captain, Pride of the Road, **Endrider Spikespitter** and **Boom Scholar**.
  **Half of them are red uncommons** — the clearest single illustration of why Boros finishes last.
  The last two matter because they're the named payoffs of Start Your Engines and the Gruul exhaust
  engine respectively, and neither delivers.
- **Monument to Endurance is a trap** — taken in the first three picks and well below the bar. The
  cycling payoff that does deliver is **Captain Howler, Sea Scourge**.
- **Elvish Refueler and Wickerfolk Thresher are high picks that change nothing when you draw them.**
- **Momentum Breaker is the real Rakdos linchpin.**
- **Push the Limit is a genuine build-around, and the field prices it correctly.** Its decks finish
  below average *and* drawing it wins games — the signature of a card that pays off when assembled
  and drags the aggregate down when not. Take it late and cheap; don't draft toward it.
- **The fixing is enabling, not picks.** Night Market and Starting Column make your deck castable and
  do nothing when drawn instead of a spell. Guidelight Matrix the field is right to leave.
- **The clunky removal is more clunky than good** — Crash and Burn, Scrap Compactor and Broken Wings
  all sit below the format baseline.
- **Riverchurn Monument is one of the best blue rares**, not a curiosity mill card.
- **Big White is real, and the aggressive version is the trap.** Marshals' Pathcruiser and Ride's End
  carry it, Guidelight Pathmaker supports it, and Air Response Unit does not.
- **The wheel is shallower here than in most sets.** Spectral Interference, Maximum Overdrive,
  Broadcast Rambler, Bounce Off, Hulldrifter and Voyager Quickwelder all come back, but they're
  serviceable rather than genuinely underpriced.

### Open questions the data can't settle

- **How good the Push the Limit ceiling really is.** The aggregate can't see an assembled
  build-around, and drawing the card demonstrably does something. The guide is describing the built
  deck; the number is describing every attempt at it. Both are true — and since the card is cheap,
  the attempt is low-cost.
- **How fast the format actually is — the two sources genuinely disagree.** This brief's existing
  read, and the calibration at the top of the page, is that DFT is slower than a vehicle set has any
  right to be, board stalls are common, and you answer resolved threats rather than racing them.
  LLU's post-format read is that DFT is "combat-centric" and "you're racing a decent amount," with
  failure to affect the board on turns two through four snowballing games away. Neither source is
  predicting; both are describing a finished format. The likeliest reconciliation is that the
  *clock* is slow but the *tempo penalty* is steep — games go long, but the player who spent turns
  two through four doing nothing has already lost by the time they get there. Until someone settles
  it, the safe build satisfies both: an on-curve board presence early, and the ability to answer a
  resolved threat late.
- **Green versus black for best colour.** The retrospective hedges and the data has green ahead by a
  real but small margin, while black's commons are genuinely the deeper set. It rarely matters, since
  the top three archetypes are all green anyway.
