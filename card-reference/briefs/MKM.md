## Format brief — everything that isn't a single card

Every number lives on the card tiles below and in the archetype table above. This brief is the other
half: what three expert guides worked out that a win-rate column structurally cannot say — how to
sequence a turn, which removal spell actually answers what, how many lands to run, and why a card
plays better or worse than it rates.

**Sources:** Limited Resources (six episodes ending in the #743 Sunset Show), NumotTheNummy (21 VODs
across the full format), Lords of Limited (six episodes ending in the 2024-03-25 retrospective). No
Limited Level-Ups guide exists for this set. All three had played the format out when they recorded
their retrospectives, so their gameplay reads are well-tested — and where they disagree, the finished
data on the tiles settles it.

**One calibration to carry into the tiles:** MKM's win rates run tight, so small differences between
commons are mostly noise. What separates them is whether drawing the card changed the game — and in
this format those two measures disagree unusually often. See "Where the field misprices cards".

### What the data says

*(The full archetype table renders directly above this brief.)*

**Boros is the best deck and the most-drafted deck at once**, clear of second while a fifth of the
field is in it — a contested lane that wins anyway. Every source called it. White's four pairs finish
1st, 2nd, 4th and 5th, so **being predisposed to white is a real heuristic**, not a slogan.

**Colour order: green and white tied at the top, then red, then blue, then black** — but black is
barely behind blue, so "avoid black" overstates it. What black *is* short of is a good archetype: its
three pairs are the bottom three.

**Where to sit when white is gone: Simic is the best non-white pair and it is under-drafted.** That
is the open seat. **Golgari and Dimir are the traps** — drafted far more than their results justify.

**The rares decide games:** Aurelia's Vindicator, Izoni, Vein Ripper, Cryptic Coat, Doppelgang and
Teysa are the top tier. Black is the worst colour *and* holds two of the six best cards — which is
exactly why drafting black for a bomb works while drafting black speculatively does not.

### The draft plan

1. **Be predisposed to white; look for reasons to stay rather than exit.** Four pairs in the top five.
2. **Have a turn-two play, and win the die roll.** The most-confirmed claim across all three guides,
   and nothing in the data argues. Not affecting the board on two of your first three turns loses to
   *most* decks, not just to aggro.
3. **Take cheap instant interaction** — roughly three efficient removal spells minimum, since it is
   the scarcest late-pack resource. Benchmarks: **Long Goodbye**, **Extract a Confession** (better
   than Murder, as all three guides say) and **Bite Down on Crime**.
4. **Take the bomb rare when you open it, especially in black.** Good drafters avoid black, so black
   bombs flow late. Committing to black *for a bomb* is the play; committing speculatively is not.
5. **The engine of the best deck is its commons, not its signposts.** Boros runs on **Novice
   Inspector** — the best common in the set — plus Makeshift Binding, Person of Interest, Dog Walker
   and Case of the Gateway Express. Its gold signpost, Meddling Youths, actively loses games.

### What the numbers can't tell you

- **Re-evaluate every removal spell through ward 2.** A face-down disguise creature is a ward-2 2/2,
  so a one-mana burn spell is often a three-mana kill. Removal that *sidesteps* ward is worth more
  than its rate suggests: fights (Bite Down on Crime), edicts (Extract a Confession), and −X/−X
  (Slice from the Shadows) don't pay the tax the same way.
- **Fire instant-speed removal in response to the face-down play**, not after it flips. This is the
  single most repeated self-diagnosed punt in Numot's VODs.
- **Check Watchdog before you plan a flip** — it stops *you* turning creatures face up, and it is
  easy to forget even when you know the text.
- **Hold priority for artifact and clue triggers.** Activate a sacrifice outlet before a token
  auto-sacrifices; crack a Clue before its draw resolves when you want to chain off an empty hand.
  Arena's auto-pass throws this value away silently.
- **Don't trust the auto-tapper** when you have flip costs, activated abilities, or a trick to hold
  up — it has cost lines outright.
- **Combat tricks beat removal more often than usual**, because the threat of a face-down flip makes
  defenders play scared. Two of the three guides land on this independently.
- **Just commit your board.** Every wrath in the set is rare or mythic and Clue card advantage means
  both players rebuild, so over-committing is rarely the losing line without a specific read.
- **Don't sandbag a bomb.** Casting the game-winner now beats holding it for a perfect spot.
- **Evaluate cards by how many synergy boxes they check** — surveil/self-mill, creatures leaving the
  graveyard, collect-evidence enabling, investigate. A card that does three of them beats a card with
  a better rate that does one.

### Deckbuilding doctrine

- **17 lands default. 16 only with an ultra-low curve and no real top end; 18 for slower value and
  control builds.**
- **Cap tap-lands at about two in anything aggressive.** A tapped turn two is real lost equity in the
  fastest window of the game.
- **Play the mediocre two-drop anyway.** Good two-drops are scarce and four-drops are everywhere; a
  turn-two play you don't love beats no turn-two play. Just don't take them early.
- **Collect Evidence is free — don't warp picks for it.** The cards are priced to cast without it and
  the graveyard fills from ordinary trading.
- **A single-pip splash wants about four sources**, and green does the fixing heavy lifting.
- **Escape Tunnel is the fixing worth having** — the most-played nonbasic in the set — but it enables
  your deck rather than winning you games. Don't spend a real pick on it.

### Where the field misprices cards

- **Taken early, and shouldn't be.** **Connecting the Dots** and **Assemble the Players** are the two
  worst early picks in the set by a distance — one a rare draw engine that reads well, the other a
  top-of-library payoff. Then Case of the Pilfered Proof, Officious Interrogation, **Delney** (a card
  that is routinely first-picked and loses games), Mentor of the Meek, Meddling Youths and Suspicious
  Detonation.
- **Wheels, and shouldn't** — cheap blue interaction is the recurring bargain: Reasonable Doubt,
  Fanatical Strength, Festerleech, Rubblebelt Maverick, Unauthorized Exit, Deduce, Vitu-Ghazi
  Inspector.
- **"Good decks run this" is not "this wins games."** Seasoned Consultant and Museum Nightwatch are
  the format's filler two-drops: play them, don't pick them early. **On the Job** is the same shape
  despite being framed as Boros' finisher — and it wheels.
- **The reverse shape — mediocre on average, real when drawn — means "right card, wrong deck."**
  Insidious Roots and Chalk Outline are the graveyard build-arounds: genuine cards, but the decks
  assembled around them were too slow for the format.
- **Crimestopper Sprite is a bust;** Forensic Researcher is the better card at a similar price.
- **Public Thoroughfare is close to unplayable.** The other fixing is fine.
- **Jaded Analyst is not a defensive all-star.**
- **Three cards wheel because the field is right about them**, despite reputations as late-format
  overperformers: Red Herring, Felonious Rage, Goblin Maskmaker. The aggro decks that ran them were
  good; the cards weren't.
- **The Special Guests bonus sheet carries no expert notes** — Enlisted Wurm, Smuggler's Copter,
  Crashing Footfalls. Their tiles rest on a tiny fraction of the games a common's does, so read them
  with much wider error bars.

### Open questions the data can't settle

- **Dog Walker's exact ceiling.** Consensus best hybrid common; one host flags it as over-rated by the
  community. The numbers split it — it is among the very best commons by win rate, but drawing it
  barely moves a game. A card that makes decks rather than wins them: very high pick in Boros,
  ordinary elsewhere.
- **Pauper or Prince.** One retrospective settles on Pauper — disguise blunts bombs enough that an
  all-common low-curve Boros deck beats a good rare. The other calls it a deep, fair rare pool where
  rares define games. Both are supported: the top rares clear the top commons by a wide margin, *and*
  the best archetype needs none of them.
