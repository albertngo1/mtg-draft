## Format brief — everything that isn't a single card

> The colours here are close enough that colour-level differences are near noise; what separates
> cards is whether drawing one changed the game. The Limited Resources source names 37 of 281 cards
> and carries three letter grades in total — authority on the *format*, silent on individual cards,
> and its silence is coverage rather than a verdict.

### What the data says

*(The full archetype table renders directly above this brief.)*

**The two white pairs are the best archetypes, and the graveyard decks are not.** That is the single
most useful thing on this page, because DSK reads like a graveyard format — Delirium, Manifest Dread
and the Eerie/reanimator shells are what the set is *about*. But **Golgari, Orzhov and Dimir all
finish in the bottom half**, while Boros and Azorius — the two pairs least entangled with the
graveyard — take the top two spots and a third of the metagame between them.

**The bottom four pairs are genuinely bad and genuinely rare.** The format is top-heavy: six real
decks and four that aren't.

**A second source, drawn a year later, reaches nearly the same table.** Limited Resources' flashback
tier list puts exactly two pairs in its top tier — **Azorius enchantment tempo and Boros aggro** —
and names Selesnya, Orzhov, Izzet and Dimir as its bottom four, with Selesnya worst. That is the
archetype table above, arrived at independently. **The one place the two disagree is Golgari**: LR
calls the green Delirium decks "all quite good," a small step behind the top two, while the data has
BG in the bottom half. That disagreement is live — see the open question at the end.

**Colour order: white, blue and red bunched at the top, green just behind, black last.** Black drags,
and its pairs are scattered through the bottom of the table.

**The best uncommons are white and cheap.** **Sheltered by Ghosts** is the best card in the set
outside the rares, then Midnight Mayhem, Unnerving Grasp, Optimistic Scavenger, Oblivious Bookworm,
Under the Skin, **Disturbing Mirth** (which wheels) and Gremlin Tamer.

**Best commons:** Glimmerburst, **Trapped in the Screen**, Scorching Dragonfire, Spineseeker
Centipede, Glassworks // Shattered Yard, Unable to Scream, and **Final Vengeance** — which comes back
later than it should.

### The draft plan

1. **Default to a white pair — Boros or Azorius.** They are the two best archetypes and between them
   a third of the metagame, which means both are contested and both win anyway.
2. **Take the payoff, never the enabler.** This is the single most transferable rule either source
   states, and it holds across every archetype in the set. Wizards seeded the enablers into the
   commons — a large share of the red and green creatures happen to be artifacts, Glimmer tokens are
   enchantment creatures, Manifest Dread fills the yard for free — so Delirium and
   enchantment-matters turn on incidentally in a deck that never drafted for them. The scarce card is
   the one that *rewards* you. Too much enabler is survivable; not enough payoff is not.
3. **Take cheap exile removal above its rate.** Putting things in graveyards *helps* your opponent in
   this format, so exile and tuck effects are worth a premium. Trapped in the Screen, Sheltered by
   Ghosts, and Scorching Dragonfire's exile clause are the benchmarks.
4. **Four toughness is the magic number.** The two premier cheap removal spells are a deal-3 and a
   −3/−3, so a body above that line dodges a huge share of the format's interaction. Prioritise
   creatures that live through it, and expect your own deal-3 effects to underperform their
   reputation.
5. **Take powerful mono-coloured cards early and commit late.** Both hosts land here: starting a
   colour pair too early traps you in a weak seat. Exception — open a signpost bomb with signals that
   it's wide open, and commit immediately. **LR sharpens this by colour:** a green first pick leaves
   GU, GB and GR all open and all live, which makes green the most flexible start in the format even
   though it isn't the strongest colour on the table above. A black first pick is the opposite — UB
   and WB are poor and BR is on rails, so opening black effectively picks your deck for you. At
   close-to-equal power, prefer the green card.
6. **You need a turn-two play.** The format's must-answer two-drops — Gremlin Tamer, Irreverent
   Gremlin, Fear of Infinity — punish a slow start hard enough that hands without one are a mulligan
   question on the draw.

### What the numbers can't tell you

- **Artifacts are the hardest Delirium type to get naturally.** That is the whole practical difficulty
  of the mechanic, and it's why the "glue" cards matter more than their rate suggests — the artifact
  equipment, the fetch-land, the artifact-creature bodies, and the common that puts a sorcery plus a
  creature-or-land in the yard in one shot.
  **The sources disagree here, and it is worth knowing both.** LR's retrospective argues close to the
  opposite: the artifact type is nearly free, because so many ordinary red and green creatures happen
  to *be* artifacts, so a body that trades in combat quietly advances Delirium without you planning
  it. The two reads reconcile if you split by colour — the artifact type is close to automatic in a
  red or green deck and genuinely hard outside one. Base RG or BG: trust LR and don't spend picks on
  glue. Reaching for Delirium from anywhere else: the original warning stands.
- **Manifest Dread does two things at once and you choose which.** A face-down 2/2 you can flip later
  *and* a card into the graveyard for Delirium. Which half you need changes turn to turn — that is
  the read a win rate can't show you. **One thing hindsight settles:** actually paying to turn a
  face-down creature up "wasn't a huge part of the card — it did happen sometimes, but mostly it did
  not." Price Manifest Dread as a 2/2 plus a self-mill trigger, and treat the flip as upside you will
  often never use.
- **Glimmer tokens make board development and payoff-triggering the same action.** A 1/1 Glimmer is
  an enchantment creature, so the card that makes one is paying you twice: a body now, and a trigger
  for every enchantment-matters permanent you already control. That is why the Glimmer-makers
  outperform their text in a way a rate comparison can't show, and why Azorius is a tempo deck rather
  than a durdle deck.
- **Ramp is better here than in most sets, because there is so much to do with the mana.** Unlocking
  rooms, activating Manifest Dread, flipping face-down creatures and casting the expensive payoffs
  all compete for the same mana, so a deck that reaches five and six early converts the surplus
  immediately. This is a second, independent reason the green fixing creatures rate highly — they
  ramp as well as fix.
- **Rooms are a deckbuilding commitment, not a free inclusion.** They count as enchantments for Eerie
  and as permanents that left the battlefield for the sacrifice payoffs, but a deck that wants them
  has to actually be built for them. **Hindsight adds the reason the dedicated rooms deck failed:**
  most rooms don't affect the board on the turn you spend the mana, and in a format whose two best
  decks are board-centric that is fatal. Rooms as incidental enchantments, yes. Rooms as the plan,
  no.
- **Exiling versus killing matters more here than almost anywhere.** Against a Delirium or reanimator
  opponent, a kill spell is doing part of their work. Read the removal on your tiles with that in mind.
- **Survival triggers in the second main phase**, which rewards attacking and snowballs — but see
  below on how that actually worked out.
- **Plan your splash early.** Green gets extra fixing on top of the colourless fixing everyone has,
  which is what makes three-colour graveyard piles reachable. Boros is the one archetype that does
  **not** want to splash and benefits least from the options.

### Deckbuilding doctrine

- **16–17 lands.** With the fetch-land, the clunky land-cyclers at common in every colour, and
  Manifest Dread smoothing, 16 is genuinely available in a two-colour deck with fixing.
- **Three colours is normal in the green decks, and Temur is a real deck.** The common dual-land
  cycle plus the green fixing creatures make the green-based Delirium and graveyard piles genuinely
  fluid — the two standard builds are UG splashing red for cheap burn, or RG splashing blue for a
  card or two. **The one combination to avoid is RGW.** Everything else is reachable off a green
  start without much cost.
- **Two archetypes don't splash at all: Boros and Rakdos.** Boros because it wants the cheap curve
  intact; Rakdos because it is on rails and doesn't want other colours' cards anyway. That rigidity
  is also Rakdos's argument — you are usually the only drafter at the table competing for its
  payoffs.
- **Don't build the graveyard deck by default.** The mechanics point that way and the archetype
  results do not — commit to Delirium or reanimator only when the lane is open and you have multiple
  payoffs, not because the set is themed around it.

### Where the field misprices cards

- **Taken early, and shouldn't be.** **Dazzling Theater // Prop Room is the worst early pick in this
  entire reference** — taken in the first five picks and near the bottom of the set. Behind it:
  Doomsday Excruciator, Maddening Hex, Savior of the Small, Charred Foyer // Warped Space, Orphans of
  the Wheat, Fear of Exposure, Marvin, Patched Plaything, **Kona, Rescue Beastie** (a top-three pick
  that loses games) and Meathook Massacre II.
- **Wheels, and shouldn't** — cheap interaction again: **Commune with Evil**, Vanish from Sight, Turn
  Inside Out, Innocuous Rat, Twist Reality, Winter's Intervention, Don't Make a Sound, Friendly Ghost.
- **Selesnya's Survival package is where the theme failed.** The pair finishes near the bottom and its
  two signposts, Baseball Bat and Shrewd Storyteller, are the evidence. The mechanic snowballs when it
  works, and the decks built for it didn't.
- **Roaring Furnace // Steaming Sauna is the best Room in the set by a distance**, and Izzet finishing
  ninth is not its fault.
- **Nowhere to Run is black's best card** and one of the two removal spells that defines the
  four-toughness line.
- **Threats Around Every Corner is the green uncommon that actually delivers;** Wickerfolk Thresher is
  the one taken far above its worth.
- **Terramorphic Expanse is fixing plus a Delirium type, not a high pick.** It enables; it doesn't win.
- **Rite of the Moth and Miasma Demon are the Orzhov reanimator signposts**, and Orzhov is near the
  bottom. The reanimator plan is available and it is not good.
- **Late for a reason:** Cracked Skull, Cautious Survivor, Intruding Soulrager, Ghostly Keybearer.
- **Pyroclasm is a sideboard card, not a maindeck sweeper** — it kills most of your own board outside
  a dedicated control build.

### Open questions the data can't settle

- **Is the graveyard theme worth building for at all?** The mechanics are deep, the payoffs are real
  card-for-card (Drag to the Roots, Broodspinner, Under the Skin), and the archetypes still finish in
  the bottom half. The most likely reconciliation is the one the guides gesture at: the graveyard
  cards are good, the *dedicated* decks are too slow, and the right amount of Delirium is
  "incidental" rather than "built-around." The aggregate cannot separate those two builds, so this
  stays open. **The new source pushes on this from the other side.** LR ranks the green Delirium
  decks — RG, BG and GU — as a clear second tier, calls RG the host's personal favourite, and reports
  a four-figure Arena Open finish with it, while agreeing that the *dedicated* theme decks (GW
  Survival, WB reanimator) failed. So the split may not be "graveyard versus not" at all but "green
  Delirium midrange versus the two non-green graveyard archetypes." That still leaves BG's bottom-half
  finish unexplained, which is why this is an open question and not a resolution.
- **How aggressive is the format really?** The guides call it midrange and note that five- and
  six-drop uncommons are legitimate early picks, yet the two winning archetypes are the two white
  tempo pairs. Treat the format as midrange in card quality and tempo in tactics. **LR's retrospective
  lands in the same place** — "not the fastest set, but the two best archetypes were fairly
  assertive" — which is corroboration from a settled vantage point rather than a new claim.
- **What happens at four mana.** LR is blunt that four-drop ground creatures "really fell off," that
  three toughness was a fault line in the set, and that you should keep your curve low. Lords of
  Limited, drafting the format live, treats five- and six-drop uncommons as legitimate early picks.
  Both can hold — an expensive card that does something on arrival is not a vanilla four-mana 3/4 —
  but nobody has drawn the line, and the two sources would advise you differently on the same pack.
