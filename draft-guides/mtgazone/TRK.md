# TRK — MTG Arena Zone Limited Set Review (Star Trek)

Per-card review by **J2SJosh** (The Draft Lab), published 2026-10-30 to 2026-11-02 across seven
articles (five colours, multicolor, artifacts and lands), captured 2026-11-11. Ratings are the
reviewer's own **0–5**; they are held in [grades/mtgazone_TRK.json](../../grades/mtgazone_TRK.json)
and render as the **AZ** column.

⚠ **Pre-release opinion.** Published before TRK's release, so every rating is a prediction made with
no games played. 17Lands GIH WR supersedes all of it once the set is on Arena.

The Planets, Shock Lands and Surveil Lands were reviewed as three groups (2.5, 3.0 and 2.5); each
land in a group carries that grade in the JSON but has no note here. The Stardates bonus sheet is
not covered by this review.

Notes below are the reviewer's own words, trimmed to the first sentences of each review.

## Card notes

- **70,000 Light-Years from Home** — I have successfully resisted the temptation to turn this into a Buzz Lightyear reference. You should all be proud of me. I do appreciate that the land goes into their hand instead of directly onto the battlefield, but let’s not pretend giving your opponent a card is suddenly a good thing.
- **A Good Day to Die** — I’m not particularly impressed with this one, even at the bargain price of a single mana. Cheap combat tricks are usually something I’m happy to consider, but they still need to actually accomplish something worthwhile.
- **Academy Instructor** — Now this is the kind of instruction the spells deck needs. Every noncreature spell leaves behind a 1/1, letting you build an army or chump block while you get up to your usual shenanigans. Better yet, you’re getting rewarded for casting cards the deck already wants: removal, card draw, and tricks.
- **Adversary by Algorithm** — We’ve come a long way from the days when Call of the Herd dominated Constructed. It would still be fantastic in Limited today, which makes it a useful benchmark here. Adversary by Algorithm falls a little short because of the Beam Me Up requirement.
- **All Good Things . . .** — All good things must come to an end, including drawing extra cards every turn. Apparently, unlimited value is too much to ask for these days. Fortunately, this lets you sift through an absolutely obnoxious number of cards before the fun stops.
- **Amok Time** — Considering this article should be going up on Halloween (if its late, I blame gremlins and not the Gizmo kind), it’s only appropriate that I sneak in a Hocus Pocus reference. “Amok! Amok, amok, amok, amok, amok!” I’d say back to Magic, but technically those witches qualify as Magic too.
- **Andromedan Automatons** — These are a nightmare to grade properly because their value is going to fluctuate wildly depending on how many copies you manage to draft. This is one of those cards where reading the table is almost as important as reading the card. For example, let’s say you passed one at pick one, but another shows up at pick six.
- **Antagonist Incorporator** — The stats are perfectly respectable here and should usually allow this to sneak through early and poop out a baby Borg token. Who doesn’t love a little free assimilation with their morning combat? On its own, this is already a solid card that can provide some nice incremental value.
- **Anthropological Mission** — Let me check the archetypes really quick. Yup, Izzet is a spells deck again. Apparently, some traditions are simply too sacred for Wizards to mess with. That means this has a pretty obvious home in the Izzet deck, and honestly, that’s about it.
- **Armus, Skin of Evil** — This is functionally a split card, although apparently that’s not good enough to count as facing a dilemma. I guess even pure evil has to follow the rules. On one side, you’ve got a three-mana -4/-4 removal spell. On the other, you’ve got a gigantic seven-mana creature that’s going to tear apart your opponent’s hand.
- **Artificial Anomaly** — A colorless land is a genuine cost in Limited, so it had better earn its spot. Artificial Anomaly does not make a particularly persuasive case. Once you’re at five mana, including black, you can eventually use the ability to drain two life. Thrilling.
- **Assault Drone** — Assault Drone lives and dies by the number of artifact creatures you’ve managed to cram into your deck. If you’ve got absolutely no other artifact creatures running around, this is probably closer to a 1.0. A 3/4 for four that does nothing else isn’t a card that you should be playing.
- **Assembly Specialist** — Getting two 1/1 dorks for two mana is pretty much the going rate these days. It’s not exciting, but spreading your stats across multiple bodies has always had some value in Limited. Where this gets more interesting is in a dedicated Borg deck.
- **Astute Terraformer** — It would be astute not to play too many of these in your average Limited deck. A 2/2 that can turn into a 4/4 sounds reasonable until you remember you’re spending four mana on the upgrade. For that kind of investment, I expect Bane pumping himself full of Venom, not a Grizzly Bears buying a gym membership.
- **Automated Warfare System** — I suspect this will find a home in plenty of Limited decks, even if the name is overselling how eager I am to go to war with it. A Wind Drake is still a serviceable low-end playable when evasion matters.
- **Bat'leth** — I know some people are going to compare this to Crude Bent Blade, but I don’t think those two cards are even in the same galaxy. Sure, getting to target a specific creature is nice, but giving something -2/-2 is considerably worse than forcing a sacrifice when you’re paying three mana for the privilege.
- **Battle-Scarred Survivalist** — A dedicated sacrifice deck could grow this into something worth the investment. Everywhere else, you’re starting with an unimpressive creature and hoping the rest of your deck comes to the rescue. That floor is low enough to make me wary before we even get to the restrictions.
- **Beckett Mariner, Impetuous Ensign** — This is basically a two-mana 2/1 first striker wearing a paragraph of extra rules text. Reading all those words doesn’t magically improve the card. What do you get for managing to attack with Mariner three times? A whopping two extra damage to your opponent’s face.
- **Blastoneuron** — Reusing one of your better enters-the-battlefield effects while adding another body is a tempting package, especially if your deck has a few creatures worth bringing back. The drain ability might steal a game here and there, but I wouldn’t count on it often enough to push the grade any higher.
- **Boarding Action** — Boarding Action seems to have two competing ideas about what your deck is supposed to do. The name screams aggression: turn creatures sideways and keep the pressure on. Beam Me Up, meanwhile, asks you to give up tempo by picking up a creature you’ve already committed to the battlefield.
- **Borg Cube** — Resistance is futile. Especially when your opponent gets to exile your best creature for a fraction of the usual price. In a dedicated Borg deck, Borg Cube can be dirt cheap, giving you incredibly efficient exile removal.
- **Brad Boimler, Eager Ensign** — A 2/2 lifelinker for two is still a perfectly reasonable Magic card, even if it’s not quite the powerhouse it was back in the olden days. Power creep has not been particularly kind to our good friend Grizzly Bears, even when he comes with a little extra padding for your life total.
- **Branching Timeline** — I know I’m beating a dead horse, but these grades assume an average Limited deck. In a normal two-color build, you should absolutely not be playing this. Without enough differently named lands, the payoff simply isn’t worth the investment.
- **Brazen Bluff** — Every once in a while, a card comes along that has absolutely no business being in your Limited deck. Brazen Bluff isn’t merely situational; it needs a spectacular collection of circumstances to accomplish something mediocre.
- **Cantankerous Captain** — I’ll somehow manage to restrain myself from making the approximately 47 jokes about how “cantankerous” is just a fancy way of saying several other four-letter words. I’m sure you can fill in the blanks yourselves. As for the card, it’s a useful role-player in an aggressive deck.
- **Captain Benjamin Sisko** — Here’s another card that’s going to fluctuate pretty wildly depending on the deck you put him in. I wouldn’t count on activating that five-color ability very often, but generating a 1/1 every time you face a dilemma could actually be pretty powerful.
- **Captain James T. Kirk** — Giving Kirk a 4.0 feels right, even if the games won’t all play out like one of his legendary victories. At his floor, you’re getting a 2/2 and a 1/1 for three mana. Fine, if a little underwhelming for a man whose job description apparently includes saving the galaxy every other Tuesday.
- **Captain Jean-Luc Picard** — Before getting into the card, I want to take a moment to acknowledge the passing of Sir Patrick Stewart. For generations of Star Trek fans, Stewart wasn’t just the actor who played Captain Picard. He was the embodiment of everything that made the character great.
- **Captain Jonathan Archer** — A 3/3 that brings a 1/1 along would already be an excellent Limited card. Archer goes further: together, he and his companion contribute three toward Federation, effectively handing out a Giant Growth every turn without needing another creature. But let’s discuss the important part. That 1/1 token is Porthos.
- **Captain Kathryn Janeway** — Playing an additional land each turn is nice, but you’ll run out of lands quickly enough in an average Limited game. Janeway fixes that by letting you explore whenever a creature enters under your control, including herself. I see what you did there, Wizards.
- **Captain Michael Burnham** — Unless you can put multiple lands onto the battlefield in a turn, Burnham is a serviceable Limited creature without much to distinguish her from the crowd. Trigger her repeatedly and the math changes; now she’s a payoff rather than just another body on the curve.
- **Captain Pike, Fullness of Time** — Playing lands straight off the top of your library is an excellent way to generate card advantage. Every land you play that way is one fewer draw step spent finding mana instead of action. Pike will usually be a 2/2 or 3/3 in a normal two-color deck, and I’m happy with that when the ability is doing this much work.
- **Captain's Tutelage** — This feels like one of those combat tricks that I never actually put in my deck, but somehow end up getting absolutely wrecked by later in the format because I’ve stopped bothering to play around it.
- **Cetacean Ops** — This creature is LARPing as an opp. See, kids? I’m still cool and down with the latest slang. At least we’ve moved on from the days of calling everything “on fleek.” Those were dark times. Questionable attempts to sound like someone under the age of 40 aside, we’ve seen plenty of variations of this creature before.
- **Cha'DIch Investigator** — Believe it or not, I actually like what this card is doing. You’re not exactly getting ahead on mana when it comes to the total stats, but spreading those stats across two bodies, with part of the package having haste, is a pretty respectable deal.
- **Class-F Shuttlecraft** — There are better mana fixing options in this set, but if your deck needs another way to fix its mana, Class-F Shuttlecraft can do the job. No one’s going to first-pick a glorified mana rock, but not every card in your forty has to be exciting.
- **Cleveland Booker, Empath** — Sigh. I already wasted my Cleveland joke on another card. I suppose I can show a little empathy for Browns fans and let this one slide. They’ve suffered enough. As for Booker, I’d prefer to play him when I can immediately follow up with a land.
- **Cloak and Phaser** — In your average two-color Limited deck, this is probably going to cost around six mana. That’s assuming you’ve got one of each basic land and managed to draw a dual land along the way. Six mana is still an awful lot to pay for this effect, and things get considerably uglier if that dual land never makes an appearance.
- **Cloistered Telepath** — It shouldn’t take telepathic powers to figure out that I’d prefer this one stay cloistered in my sideboard. The biggest problem is that being a 2/1 means flash isn’t really going to let you surprise an opponent who thought they were getting in a free attack.
- **Cold-Blooded Crew** — This is in the same boat—or spaceship—as the other basic landcyclers. Need a color early? Cycle it away. Flooded late? You’ve still got a creature worth casting. The body isn’t going to win any awards, but smoothing out your mana without sacrificing a late-game play is exactly why these cards earn their spots.
- **Collation Trimatrix** — I’m assuming your average Limited deck has enough artifacts to activate this fairly regularly. If that’s true, you’ve got a serious source of repeatable card advantage. In a dedicated Borg deck, though, this thing could get absolutely bananas.
- **Collective Drone** — Does this have the potential to swing for an absolutely massive amount of damage? It sure does. Does it also have the unfortunate tendency to trade with some random two-drop and die to practically every removal spell in the format? Also yes. That’s the tradeoff.
- **Combadge** — Technically, this gives you two rectangles for the price of one. Unfortunately, they need to be equipped to two different creatures before either rectangle accomplishes anything. That’s an impressive amount of paperwork for a couple of badges.
- **Command Decision** — As we’ve seen in recent Limited formats, two mana to destroy a creature with power four or greater is actually a pretty solid deal. Sure, it’s conditional removal, but having the option to cash it in for another card when it doesn’t have a good target makes it considerably more appealing.
- **Command Subadjunct** — Remember when Savannah Lions was not only a rare, but actually saw significant Constructed play? Pepperidge Farm remembers, and unfortunately, so do I. We’ve reached the point where this is a significant upgrade over that once-powerful kitty, and it still only manages to be an alright Limited card.
- **Commander Raffi Musiker** — Remember when Phyrexian Rager was all the rage in Limited? Those were simpler times, when getting a card attached to a reasonably sized creature was enough to make everyone happy, even if it cost you a life. These days, we’ve gotten plenty of variations that don’t even charge you that life.
- **Common Goal** — This lands right around where I’d grade most two-mana bite spells. Sorcery speed hurts, but being able to use one or two creatures makes up the difference. Combining their power lets you take down something too large for either creature alone.
- **Conquest Mission** — Can this card be situationally busted? Absolutely. You just need the right deck, the right board state, and presumably the stars to align while you’re at it. This feels like a little too much of a build-around for my tastes.
- **Consider the Prime Directive** — Mana Leak is a pretty respectable Magic card. Mana Leak for three mana? Considerably less exciting. That’s already the going rate for Cancel, and these days we’re usually getting some extra value stapled onto that for our troubles. The other option isn’t exactly setting the world on fire either.
- **Constable Odo** — I suspect I’ll choose the double-investigate option more often than not. Two Clues are a nice way to keep the cards coming, particularly when the game slows down and you’ve got spare mana to spend. The other option still has its uses, though, which is what I want from a modal card.
- **Crystalline Entity** — The good: you can potentially wipe the battlefield, keep some of your own creatures, and walk away with a 7/7 flyer. That’s a pretty effective way to turn a losing game into a winning one. The bad: EIGHT. FREAKING. MANA. And casting this against a Borg deck sounds like trusting a fart after Taco Bell.
- **Culber and Stamets, Star-Crossed** — In a vacuum, this is a 4/5 for four mana. We’ve come a long way from Erhnam Djinn crushing dreams in Constructed. Putting the counter on another creature gives you some useful options, especially when you’ve got something evasive that would appreciate the extra muscle.
- **Cybernetic Specialist** — I would like this card considerably more if it had two toughness. With all the 1/1 tokens wandering around, trading your real card for somebody’s free bonus isn’t exactly an inspiring combat plan. Of course, colorless creatures come with the ‘available to every deck’ tax.
- **D'Ghor Bloodletter** — Now THIS is the kind of modality I can get behind. Instead of giving me two mediocre options and expecting me to be impressed because I get to choose between them, both modes actually do something worthwhile.
- **D'Vana Tendi, Optimistic Ensign** — I’m not nearly as optimistic about Tendi as I am about some of the other signpost uncommons. She just doesn’t line up particularly well with what Simic is trying to accomplish. Simic has apparently decided that two colors are beneath it and would rather collect every differently named land in the set.
- **D-7 Battle Cruiser** — I was ready to call this a straight-up bomb until I noticed you have to sacrifice the token at the end of every turn. That’s a pretty important distinction between building an army and renting one. I could even see choosing not to finish stationing this.
- **DOT-7 Repair Squad** — I’m not exactly the biggest Phantom Monster enthusiast in 2026. That being said, this one does have some nice synergy with stationing your Spacecraft, which gives it a little more utility than your average flying beatstick. In a deck looking to get its Spacecraft online, I can certainly see this pulling its weight.
- **Dahar Master** — You’re obviously going to want some expendable sacrifice fodder to get the most out of this. The body is slightly below curve, but turning a disposable creature, treasure, or food into a Shock on the way in makes up for it. Picking off a two-toughness threat while developing your own board is a trade I’m happy to make.
- **Dal R'El, Augment Orphan** — Dal helps you hit the gas early, then turns excess lands into real cards once another Forest becomes about as exciting as watching paint dry. That’s a useful combination. Early on, you want to hit land drops and cast your expensive spells ahead of schedule.
- **Daniels, Temporal Agent** — Gravedigger isn’t what he used to be, but I’m sure you’re all tired of hearing me complain about power creep. I’m starting to sound like an old man yelling at clouds, which, admittedly, is pretty much my brand. The bigger issue here is that single point of toughness.
- **Data, New Life** — Don’t get me wrong, there are absolutely going to be games where Data comes down on turn two, starts growing, and eventually runs away with the entire game.
- **Dathon and Picard at El-Adrel** — This is primarily a sideboard card, and even there, I’m not particularly enthusiastic. If I got to choose which artifact or enchantment to destroy, I’d happily run a copy in my main deck. There are an awful lot of juicy targets running around this format, and having an answer to them would be pretty valuable.
- **Dathon, His Heart Open** — Dathon, his heart open. Me, my draft pick wasted. A three-color card that needs you to cast additional spells afterward is already a tough sell. By the time you’ve assembled the mana, you may be behind schedule, and Dathon still wants you to have enchantments ready to play before he delivers a worthwhile payoff.
- **David and Carol Marcus, Creators** — Wizards keeps finding ways to make Manalith playable. This time it’s attached to a 1/3 body, which at least gives you something to block with while fixing your mana. Better yet, it contributes to your Strange New Worlds count.
- **De-evolve** — Apparently, removal spells have decided to de-evolve, because this feels like something we would have been playing back in the olden days of Limited. And not necessarily the good old days. It’s technically a two-mana removal spell, which sounds fantastic until you read the fine print.
- **Deanna Troi, Psychic Counsel** — It doesn’t take a psychic to figure out that this card is great. So why am I not giving this an even higher grade? Well, there are two pretty significant problems. First, Troi is an underwhelming late-game topdeck when you’ve already played most of your creatures.
- **Death Is Irrelevant** — I have a feeling approximately half of you are going to think this grade is way too high, while the other half will think I’ve completely lost my mind for not giving it a 5.0. Welcome to the wonderful world of evaluating expensive Limited bombs. I’m landing somewhere in the middle.
- **Deep Space 9** — I know Deep Space 9 can do some absurd things. I’ve even recommended eight-mana cards in this very review because a big enough effect can justify an enormous price tag. The problem is that nine mana feels about two or three turns further away than eight.
- **Deep Space Scan** — Five-color soup and an ordinary two-color deck might as well be evaluating two different cards here. If you’re playing five-color soup, then you absolutely want this in your deck. Getting a massive refill of cards is exactly the kind of payoff that makes all those questionable mana decisions worthwhile.
- **Dominion Assault Force** — A 4/4 with vigilance for four is a perfectly reasonable rectangle. It attacks, it blocks, and it doesn’t need an elaborate backstory to justify its existence. The dilemma gives you a choice between surveiling and exiling a card from a graveyard.
- **Dominion Infiltration** — That’s a lot of mana and an even larger wall of text for a card that accomplishes less than you’d think. You can only copy your opponent’s creatures, and each one can be chosen just once. If they’re not going wide, you might have only one or two worthwhile targets. And those copies disappear at the end of the turn.
- **Dominion Saboteur** — Four-mana Clone? Good Magic card. Five-mana Clone? Suddenly, I’m considerably less enthusiastic. Copying counters can make this better than your average Clone, but that’s situational. I’m not especially interested in paying an extra mana for something that might not matter.
- **Dominion Supervisor** — This feels closer to a 1.75, but I’m feeling optimistic. Don’t get used to it. A 4/2 trampler for three mana would usually be a 1.5. Being able to put the counter on another creature is nice, but the saving grace is everything with a counter gaining trample.
- **Dr. Beverly Crusher** — This is an odd one because, with a repeatable source of life gain, Beverly Crusher has the potential to go absolutely nuts. Unfortunately, there’s a pretty significant problem with that plan. On her own, she’s a severely understatted creature that doesn’t even have a way to trigger her own ability.
- **Dr. Joseph M'Benga** — The biggest problem is “other creatures.” M’Benga can protect the rest of your team, but he can’t protect himself, and your opponent is going to notice. He’s a doctor, not a bodyguard. Unfortunately, he’s going to need one because he has a giant target painted on him. The upside depends heavily on your deck.
- **Dr. Leonard "Bones" McCoy** — Eternal Witness has been incredible in Limited for decades, and restricting Bones to returning permanents doesn’t change that much. Getting a great card back while adding a creature for three mana is already fantastic. Then they gave him flash. Because that apparently wasn’t enough.
- **Dr. McCoy, Surly Surgeon** — I’m a doctor, not a miracle worker! McCoy basically lets you use +1/+1 counters as shield counters, and he’s even kind enough to bring one along to get things started. That’s a pretty interesting ability, especially if you’ve got a bunch of creatures running around with counters on them.
- **Eject the Warp Core** — Sacrificing one of your own permanents to destroy an opponent’s creature is a familiar Limited bargain. It’s usually a good one, provided you’ve got something disposable lying around. Between this set’s tokens and artifact synergies, finding sacrifice fodder shouldn’t be a major issue.
- **Elim Garak, Simple Tailor** — I’m not particularly comfortable with the way this guy is looking at me. He’s giving off some serious “I know where you live” vibes, which isn’t exactly what I’m looking for in someone who’s supposedly just a simple tailor. The card itself makes a much better impression.
- **Emergency Holographic Crew** — Emergency is definitely the right word for this card, because that’s exactly what it’s going to take for me to put this in my deck. The body doesn’t cut it unless you’re getting a Spacecraft back, and there won’t always be one in your graveyard.
- **Emergency Medical Hologram** — Well, this is certainly underwhelming. I suppose they can’t all be winners, even when they’re programmed to be medical professionals. There are some minor synergies here with a variety of themes, including +1/+1 counters, lifegain, and sacrifice.
- **Encounter the Unknown** — A slightly more expensive Pyroclasm or a Divination? Now that’s the kind of choice I can get behind. Neither effect is particularly groundbreaking on its own, but they solve two very different problems, and having both available on the same card is a pretty fantastic deal.
- **Energize** — Man, I really wish this card was an instant. Apparently, Scotty can beam you up, but only during your main phase. Two bounce effects on one card can still buy an awful lot of tempo, particularly when you’re trying to force through the last few points of damage. The sorcery speed hurts, but the effect is useful.
- **Engineered Explosives** — I was halfway through mentally comparing this card to Engineered Explosives when I finally bothered to read the name. Apparently, I’ve reached the stage of sleep deprivation where I’m comparing cards to themselves and calling it analysis. My next breakthrough will be discovering that Islands tap for blue mana.
- **Ensign Pavel Chekov** — Let’s get one thing straight right away. You’re not going to be building some glorious dedicated mill deck around Chekov. That said, there are certainly going to be games where you manage to knock out your opponent’s last five or six cards and steal a victory that you probably had no business winning.
- **Enterprise NX-01** — The obvious comparison is U.S.S. Excelsior, Cutting Edge, which we just discussed in yesterday’s article. Both give you some immediate interaction while offering the potential to turn into a flying threat later in the game.
- **Erica Ortegas, Plucky Pilot** — There are a few rare lands running around that have some nice interactions with this such as the cycle including Federation Outpost. Realistically, most of the time you’re going to be picking up a land just to replay it and maybe gain a whopping one life.
- **Excalbian Gamemaster** — I could see knocking the grade down a little if you’re “only” getting two extra bodies out of the deal, but let’s not pretend that’s some kind of tragedy. Even at the lower end of its potential, you’re getting a pretty ridiculous amount of value.
- **Exploratory Mission** — Four mana for an enchantment that doesn’t affect the battlefield is a painful investment in Limited. It had better pay you back quickly. Even getting started is awkward: cast this, play a land, and somehow have mana left to cast what you hit. That’s not likely to happen the turn you play this.
- **Federation Field Medic** — I love creatures that replace themselves, but much like anything involving the American healthcare system, it doesn’t come free. If you’ve already got a creature running around with a +1/+1 counter on it, this is a pretty great deal.
- **Federation Probe** — There’s an awful lot to like about this card without it being particularly spectacular at any one thing. It’s basically the Swiss Army knife of one-drops, assuming your Swiss Army knife was designed by Starfleet. A one-mana flyer with surveil thrown in is already a fine starting point.
- **Federation Resupply** — I actually really like the whole concept of Beam Me Up, and I’ve been pretty optimistic about the mechanic throughout these reviews. Unfortunately, that doesn’t mean I’m interested in playing every card that happens to have it.
- **Final Frontier** — I’m awfully tempted to give this a 2.0 because I can absolutely see it doing some impressive things in the right deck. The problem is that there are going to be plenty of decks that want absolutely nothing to do with it. It might be the final frontier, but for most decks, it’s going to be the final cut.
- **First Contact** — This is another reminder that Star Trek rewards drafting archetypes, not simply grabbing the best-looking cards in your colors. First Contact fits decks with graveyard interactions or valuable creatures to pick up with the Beam Me Up cards this can drop into the yard. Elsewhere, it can be thoroughly forgettable.
- **Free Borg Revolutionaries** — As we just saw in Reality Fracture, being able to grab any basic land for two mana is a pretty powerful ability.
- **Fugitive Listener** — Ravenous Rats effects traditionally cost two mana, but I’m fine with paying an extra mana for two more power. Making your opponent discard while leaving behind a creature that can trade with something relevant is a fair deal.
- **Gabriel Lorca, Mirror Menace** — Lorca can generate plenty of free value if your deck has enough ways to face a dilemma. That’s a reasonable expectation when you’ve drafted with the mechanic in mind. Better yet, you might never have to cast him.
- **Gaseous Anomaly** — Now this is an Anomaly with a payoff worth talking about. Drawing two cards is a hell of a lot better than draining two life, and when you cash in the land you’re effectively getting a three-for-one out of the exchange since it replaces the land with an New Planet token.
- **General Chang, Cold Warrior** — In a dedicated Klingon deck, this guy has the potential to go absolutely nuts. If you’re regularly sending creatures into combat and watching them die gloriously, Chang can turn all that carnage into some serious value. He’s useful outside the dedicated Klingon deck, too.
- **Genesis Device** — Oh, look. Another card clearly designed for Constructed. I’m looking at you, Commander players. It does replace itself, and as we discussed with Final Frontier, a three-mana ramp spell isn’t exactly the drizzling poops. Some green decks actively want that acceleration. The interesting part is the late game.
- **Genesis Effect** — Paying five mana to fix your colors feels backwards. By that point, you’ve probably figured out how to cast most of your deck, though I suppose it could find the last color for one of your wildly irresponsible splashes. In reality the best part about that is being able to get another landfall trigger at instant speed.
- **Georgiou, Terran Emperor** — This is the ultimate “Do you have removal?” card in the entire set. If your opponent doesn’t have a way to deal with a six-toughness creature, they can’t win. Literally. The ridiculous part is that you don’t even need to risk Georgiou in combat to take over the game.
- **Ghost of Illyria** — I’ve already made my feelings about this particular body pretty clear back when we discussed DOT-7 Repair Squad. Fortunately, Ghost of Illyria has the decency to offer some additional value along with those thoroughly unimpressive stats.
- **Gowron, Eager for Battle** — Gowron can get completely out of hand because he grows whenever creatures die on either side of the battlefield. That’s dangerous by itself, but it also creates some truly disgusting combat tricks. Imagine Gowron getting into a fight, only for you to kill an entirely different creature with a removal spell.
- **Grand Nagus Zek** — I understand the once-per-turn restriction from a Constructed perspective. Nobody needs a combo deck generating approximately 400 Treasures in a turn. In Limited, though, that safety valve makes Zek painfully slow.
- **Gray and Adira, Forever Joined** — It’s probably a good thing this ability triggers only once per turn. If every noncreature spell made another copy, we’d be discussing something completely cracked. As printed, the performance is going to swing wildly from game to game.
- **Guidance Failure** — We’ve seen approximately 300 variations of this effect over the years, and at this point, I’m pretty sure Wizards just changes a few words and calls it a new card. Not that I’m complaining, because these spells have consistently been solid Limited playables. Give me a copy or two and I’m happy.
- **Gul Dukat, Colonial Prefect** — I suspect some people are going to think this guy is perfect just because he’s a big, shiny mythic with a wall of impressive text. He’s certainly powerful, but there are packs where I’ll open him, shrug, and pass him along.
- **Gwyndala, the Progeny** — That second ability won’t come up often, but it isn’t why I’m drafting this anyway. What you’re getting is essentially a Grizzly Bears with half of its stats coming from Equipment that you can move around to other creatures.
- **H.M.S. Bounty, Unlikely Timeship** — Don’t get me wrong, if you’ve got a couple of Humpback Whales swimming around in your deck, this is a perfectly reasonable Magic card. Being able to fetch something worthwhile is always nice, especially when you’ve got some particularly large aquatic mammals waiting to make an appearance. No whales, no bueno.
- **He's Dead, Jim** — Well, he was wearing a red shirt. What did you think was going to happen? Unconditional removal is always welcome in Limited, though I’d rather pay three mana than four. The surveil is a useful consolation prize for the extra cost.
- **Hemmer, Fixing What Is Broken** — There’s not much that needs fixing here. A 2/3 for two mana that loots whenever it attacks is already a great Limited card, especially when the body can survive combat with plenty of early creatures. Spacecraft make him even better.
- **Highly Illogical** — It would be highly illogical to spend an early pick on this card. Even Spock would struggle to come up with a convincing argument for it. One of the biggest advantages of a two-mana counterspell is being able to counter your opponent’s three-drop when you’re on the draw.
- **Hit It!** — Maybe my inner Magic boomer is showing, but I remember when Fires of Yavimaya was an absolute beating. Giving your entire team haste can fundamentally change a game, and apparently I’m still not over it. Hit It! isn’t merely an excuse to reference the Beasty Boys.
- **Hive Mind Coprocessor** — The body is adequate rather than impressive, but the reason to play this is what happens when it starts attacking. Your opponent doesn’t want to let you draw cards, so they’re pushed into blocks they’d probably rather avoid.
- **Holo-Engineer** — There are decks where this is a no-questions-asked 5.0. My only hesitation is that you need other token makers to unlock everything it can do. Without that support, you’re stuck with a mere 6/6 worth of stats across two bodies for four mana. Oh, the horror.
- **Hologram Janeway** — Let’s get one thing straight: we’re not exactly lining up to play Trained Armodon in 2026. A vanilla 3/3 for three mana hasn’t been particularly exciting in a very long time, so the rest of the package really needs to do some work to justify putting this in your deck.
- **Hologram Stalker** — Five power and toughness across two bodies while Regrowthing a permanent is a substantial return. And with more graveyard shenanigans, you can potentially produce another 3/3 every turn. The catch is six mana. That’s a big investment, and you need worthwhile permanents in your graveyard for the value to materialize.
- **Horta** — I’ve sung the praises of basic landcycling enough times already. Horta follows the same formula: fix your mana early, cast a useful creature late. It won’t be the star of your deck, but reliable cards that help you actually cast your spells don’t need to be flashy.
- **Hoshi Sato, Exolinguist** — I’m always happy to get a solid creature that digs for something useful, and Hoshi does both. You’re almost always going to be looking at at least two cards, with three or four being a realistic expectation in plenty of games.
- **Humpback Whales** — As with Free Borg Revolutionaries, basic landcycling for two mana helps fix your colors and opens the door to wildly irresponsible splashes. I know better. That has never stopped me.
- **I'm a Doctor, Not a . . .** — I actually like this combat trick quite a bit, but I’ve become increasingly hesitant to give tricks higher grades unless they’re doing something truly spectacular. For a single mana, though, you’re getting a surprisingly nice package.
- **Impossible Choice** — I’m really not a fan of giving my opponent two dudes when I’m trying to use this as a removal spell. Generally speaking, the whole point of killing something is to leave your opponent with fewer creatures, not replace their giant monster with a couple of new friends.
- **In the Pale Moonlight** — The nontoken restriction is a pretty big deal here. Your opponent can’t just toss some random Officer token overboard and call it a day. They’re actually going to have to give up something relevant, which makes this considerably more appealing than your average edict effect in a format full of disposable little dudes.
- **Infinite Diversity** — Nine mana. NINE! Reaching nine mana is hard enough. Getting a worthwhile payoff afterward is a completely different problem. You need a game-changing instant or sorcery in your graveyard and enough differently typed non-Borg creatures to justify the whole production.
- **Interstellar Effigy** — At four mana, this could have been a premium common. At five, it’s going to land firmly in the “playable with limitations” category. That doesn’t seem particularly stellar to me. The five-mana slot is already crowded, and a merely reasonable creature isn’t what I want when I’m spending that much mana.
- **Isolinear Processor** — Man, I wish this were simply a mana rock without the artifact requirement. Instead, it’s a card having a full-blown identity crisis. Strange New Worlds decks would appreciate filtering away extra ramp in the late game, but doesn’t necessarily have the artifacts to use the mana ability.
- **Jadzia Dax, Living Legacy** — I’d obviously prefer if Jadzia started with a slightly more impressive body, but getting mana acceleration and a creature that grows over time on turn two is an excellent way to start snowballing a game in your favor. That’s an especially dangerous combination in Limited.
- **Jake Sisko, War Correspondent** — Jake really needs some ways to put additional lands onto the battlefield before he starts pulling his weight. If you’re just playing one land per turn and gaining a few life along the way, you’re not exactly getting an impressive return on your investment.
- **Jett Reno, Problem Solver** — Jett Reno has a bit of an identity crisis. She wants to be in the Spacecraft deck so you can easily tap her for station, but she also wants Face a Dilemma payoffs to grow into an actual threat. Getting both plans to cooperate sounds like a problem even Jett might struggle to solve.
- **Kai Winn, Faithless Leader** — Best-of-Three gives you some information about your opponent’s deck, which helps. I’m still not particularly impressed. Here’s the problem. Most Limited decks aren’t running multiple copies of the same card, so naming something you’ve already seen isn’t necessarily going to accomplish much.
- **Khaaaaaaaaaaaannn!** — Khaaaaaaaaaaaannn! Yes, I already made the reference. At this point, screaming Khan’s name is practically a set mechanic. At least the removal is good. Two damage for two mana, four for three, or six for four gives you a clean answer at several points on the curve without asking you to commit more mana than necessary.
- **Khan, Despot by Design** — Khan looks like a superstar against a battlefield full of one-toughness creatures. Against opponents with larger bodies, that first impression gets a lot less exciting.
- **Khan, Vengeful Heart** — KHHHHHAAAAAANNNNN! Now that we’ve gotten the obligatory screaming out of the way, let’s talk about the actual card. I really like the idea of turning some random piece of sacrifice fodder into two cards.
- **Kira Nerys, Firebrand** — Kira is an odd one, mostly because she seems to think we’re playing a different format. Her ability wants creatures to enter during your attack step. That means flash creatures or instant-speed token makers, neither of which is exactly a specialty of this color pair.
- **Kirk, Commanding Officer** — Unsurprisingly, Kirk is a strong signpost uncommon. He fits neatly into an aggressive curve, keeps the pressure on, and generates card advantage without asking you to take your foot off the gas.
- **Kobayashi Maru Scenario** — This feels like a significantly worse Demonic Pact, which is quite an accomplishment considering they are both actively trying to kill you. At least Demonic Pact had the decency to provide enough value that you might actually win the game before it finished you off.
- **L'Rell, Fierce Visionary** — L’Rell is exactly what an aggressive Klingon deck wants. She helps keep the pressure on by adding more bodies to the battlefield, and you even get a bit of card selection before your temporary token poofs out of existence. The truly silly games are going to involve Gowron, Eager for Battle.
- **La'An Noonien-Singh, Security** — While this is clearly designed to fit into the Boros deck, I think it has a little more flexibility than that. Realistically, any red deck planning to put a bunch of creatures onto the battlefield could find a use for La’An. On her own, though, La’An doesn’t accomplish enough.
- **Lieutenant Commander Data** — One detail pushes Data all the way to a 3.5: your opponent’s noncreature spells trigger him too. You don’t even have to do all the work yourself. In the Izzet spells deck, this has the potential to grow into an absolutely massive threat in a hurry.
- **Live Long and Prosper** — I have a feeling I’m going to hear about this grade no matter where I land. Some people will draw a mountain of cards, double their life total, and accuse me of underrating it. Others will stare at it while getting beaten to death and ask why I rated it this high.
- **Locutus, the One Who Speaks** — I’m torn between 3.5 and 4.0 because the efficiency is absurd. Locutus distributes an awful lot of potential stats for two mana, but he’s also fragile, and there isn’t any secret card advantage waiting for you if your opponent kills him. The key detail is that he doesn’t say other artifact creatures.
- **Lore, Positronic Provocateur** — I’m really closer to a 1.75, but triggering Face a Dilemma is enough to get the grade rounded up. I’m feeling generous today. Don’t get used to it. Most of the time this is a 2/3 for three mana that surveils two. If your deck has graveyard synergies, great.
- **Lt. Cmdr. Geordi La Forge** — Geordi can get your Spacecraft stationed in a hurry, and by that I mean an absolutely ridiculous hurry. On his own, he’s potentially dropping six station counters for a three-drop.
- **Lwaxana Troi, Charming Guest** — On her own, Lwaxana is basically a Grizzly Bears that requires an extra click to untap on Arena. Apparently, we’ve reached the point where even our mediocre two-drops come with additional administrative work. There’s upside if you’ve drafted a few triggered abilities worth reusing.
- **MACO Strike Team** — A Savannah Lions isn’t impressing anyone in 2026, especially with so many 1/1 tokens running around. A one-mana 2/1 needs something extra before I’m excited. MACO Strike Team delivers. Every time your opponent targets one of your creatures with a spell or ability, you get a 1/1.
- **Make It So** — Most decks will get a serviceable modal spell out of this. If you’ve drafted plenty of Face a Dilemma payoffs, there’s a little more reason to get excited. I suspect you’ll be choosing the creature and the cards about 80% of the time.
- **Malfunctioning Holodeck** — This is approximately the 300th variation of Oblivion Ring we’ve seen over the years, and you know what? They’ve pretty much always been good. I don’t see any reason to believe this one is going to be different.
- **Marooned** — Four mana to lock down a creature without turning off its abilities isn’t a fantastic rate. Fortunately, that’s not the mode I plan to use most often. For one mana, you can lock down a creature that’s already tapped. Now we’re talking!
- **Melkotian Adjudicator** — The floor is rough, but facing a single dilemma gets this up to where it needs to be. Anything after that starts looking genuinely good. You don’t even have to warp your deck around it. There are enough playable modal spells in the format that you should be able to trigger this with cards you wanted anyway.
- **Miles O'Brien, Transporter Ops** — As we’ve discussed approximately 47 times throughout these reviews, there are plenty of ways to abuse bouncing your own creatures back to your hand with Beam Me Up. O’Brien even gets around the no-beaming restriction on Marooned, which isn’t going to come up all that often, but it’s a nice little bonus when it does.
- **Mirror Universe Intrusion** — Three mana to Clone your opponent’s best creature is an excellent deal. Give the copy menace, and suddenly your version might be even better version than the original. Sure, I’d normally prefer the option to copy my own creatures. At this price, I’ll live with the restriction.
- **Moopsy** — This is probably closer to a 2.25, but I’m giving it the bump because Moopsy is an absolutely fantastic name. Sometimes the scientific evaluation process needs to make room for a little Moopsy appreciation.
- **Mr. Spock, Science Officer** — I’m genuinely torn between giving Spock a 4.0 and a 4.5. The upside is enormous, but so is the target he paints on himself the moment he hits the battlefield. Power isn’t the issue. The question is whether you get to use any of it before your opponent points a removal spell at him.
- **Mugato** — In the immortal words of Smokey from Friday, “Daaaaammmmmnnnnn!” That’s an awful lot of stats and abilities for the price. We’ve become so obsessed with creatures drawing cards or generating tokens that it’s easy to forget the value of simply putting an enormous pile of meat onto the battlefield.
- **Munitions Enthusiast** — This person clearly looks like a perfectly sane and well-adjusted individual. I’m sure giving them access to explosives is a fantastic idea. The card itself holds up better than its owner’s judgment.
- **Neelix, Moral Support** — A card needs more than moral support to make my Limited deck, and Neelix isn’t giving me much reason to change my mind. Yes, two mana gets you two pieces of cardboard, but a Food token and a 1/1 are both pretty low on the list.
- **Nog, Ambitious Cadet** — Nog begins as a 2/2 with menace for two, which is already respectable. But apparently being an acceptable two-drop isn’t nearly ambitious enough. For one more mana, he becomes a 3/4 that surveils two when he attacks. That’s an absurd early-game sequence.
- **Nyota Uhura, Communications** — This is going to depend somewhat on your opponent’s deck, but I suspect you’ll be naming two or three most of the time. Those are pretty popular spots on the mana curve, and making your opponent miserable for playing perfectly normal Magic cards is always a strategy I can get behind.
- **Oberth-Class Starship** — This replaces itself when you cast it, so it can’t be all that bad. Unfortunately, that additional value comes with a pretty hefty station requirement for what ultimately amounts to a fairly unimpressive creature.
- **Obsidian Order Operative** — This belongs in a slower deck that’s happy to sit back and face dilemmas. There, it has a job to do. Take away the modal spells, though, and the Operative is dangerously close to unplayable. You need a steady supply of triggers before it deserves a slot.
- **Open Communications** — I think this is going to overperform, and the reason is simple: one mana. It’s cheap, it replaces itself, and it lets you reuse an enters-the-battlefield ability. You don’t need to spend half your turn assembling some elaborate Beam Me Up combo to get value here.
- **Organic Avulsion Unit** — Giving one creature menace is perfectly fine, but giving your entire army of artifact creatures menace? Now that’s something I can get behind. In a Borg deck, this can turn combat into an absolute nightmare. Good luck blocking a swarm of little artifact creatures when every single one demands two blockers.
- **Overflow Submatrix** — Ideally, you want to play this when you’ve got enough mana left over to immediately cast something else and guarantee some value. That said, it’s not the end of the world if you have to raw dog this onto the battlefield on turn three and hope for the best.
- **Pah-Wraith** — Good luck blocking this thing. It’s typically going to be attacking as at least a six-power first striker, which is already an absolute nightmare to deal with in combat. Pah-Wraith also pumps the rest of your attacking team, turning a board of ordinary creatures into a very real threat.
- **Pattern Buffer Preservation** — I expect some people to be lower on this, and I don’t want a pile of copies. The first one, though, interests me quite a bit if I’ve got creatures worth hitting with Beam Me Up. Card selection helps you hit a land early or find something relevant late.
- **Peace in Our Time** — I actually like this card quite a bit out of the sideboard when you’re playing a controlling deck against an aggressive opponent. It puts them in the rather awkward position of having to choose between gaining card advantage and actually being able to attack you.
- **Perils of the Past** — This 2.0 is for the first copy. A second copy starts drifting toward the 1.5 bin where mediocre combat tricks go to collect dust. Between the Borg and Spacecraft, there should be plenty of excellent artifacts to blow up. Two mana at instant speed is an efficient answer to some genuinely scary cards.
- **Pet Sehlat** — I’m not entirely convinced this grade is low enough. In modern Limited, if you’re going to saddle a three-drop with a drawback, you’d better be giving me some pretty impressive stats to make up for it. Unfortunately, that’s not what we’re getting here.
- **Phlox, Unorthodox Doctor** — Phlox needs help. He isn’t a card you can toss into any random pile and expect great things from, and he does very little when your battlefield isn’t cooperating. But in a dedicated +1/+1 counters deck? Good grief.
- **Photosynthetic Physician** — There are only so many five-drops you can fit into a Limited deck, and reach alone doesn’t move this one to the front of the line. The interesting part is being able to turn it into a 1/1 even after milling or discarding it. That’s useful in a graveyard-focused deck, but hardly enough to get excited about on its own.
- **Picard, Leading by Example** — We’ve already established that Grizzly Bears aren’t exactly setting the world on fire in modern Limited. That doesn’t mean you can just ignore your two-drop slot, though. You still need creatures that can get onto the battlefield early and hopefully do something useful once the game goes longer.
- **Planetary Bombardment** — One mana to kill a small creature is already solid. After combat, when you can deal four damage instead, this starts answering threats that usually require a much more expensive removal spell. Timing matters, but the upside is enormous for a single red mana.
- **Planetary Patrol** — At least they remembered to put these guys in red shirts. Nobody’s going to be surprised when they don’t survive the episode. Curve filler. Moving on.
- **Plasma Cascade** — I’d love this at two mana, but being able to hit any target makes the higher price easier to accept. And here’s your friendly reminder to check your opponent’s life total before automatically pointing this at some random creature.
- **Primary Unimatrix Adjunct** — Giving itself double strike already makes this a perfectly “reasonable” body for the cost, and by reasonable, I mean something your opponent probably isn’t going to enjoy dealing with. Where things really start getting out of hand is when you begin handing out double strike to your other artifact creatures.
- **Pure Energy** — I’ve never been particularly fond of cards that distribute +1/+1 counters like this. They tend to look considerably better on paper than they actually play, especially when you’re spending a card just to make creatures you already have slightly bigger.
- **Q, Stacking the Deck** — Stacking the deck? I believe there are a few professional Magic players who could teach Q a thing or two about that particular strategy… Q could be an unbeatable bomb that makes your opponent wonder why they bothered showing up. He could also be a 4/4 that immediately dies to Planetary Bombardment.
- **Quark, Humble Proprietor** — I’m sure there’s some absolutely ridiculous nonsense you can pull off with this card in Constructed, especially in Commander, where people have apparently decided that Magic is more fun when everyone has a 45-minute turn. One-on-one Limited is less hospitable to that nonsense.
- **Ravenous Cosmozoan** — I’m sure the Izzet spells deck is going to love this thing. I’m also pretty sure it has absolutely no business showing up in most other decks.
- **Reckless Impulse** — Reckless Impulse sounds like a pretty accurate description of how I ended up writing all these articles again. Either that, or I desperately need to fund my wife’s Disney habit. We’ve seen the card enough times to know the deal: two mana for access to two more cards.
- **Red Alert!** — Trumpet Blast effects want you to go wide, turn everything sideways, and end the game before your opponent can count the blockers. That’s what makes the defensive discount so strange. A mass pump spell isn’t usually what I’m looking for when I’m the one trying to survive.
- **Relentless Drednok** — Relentless Drednok doesn’t offer enough for the average Limited deck. It’s not embarrassing, but I’m not spending an early pick on it either. A dedicated artifact deck is a different story. With enough synergy, it can become a legitimate contributor rather than filler.
- **Replicator** — I’ll admit that Replicator needs support. Paying two mana every turn to copy a Food token is fine: a little life, some sacrifice fodder, and no particular reason for your opponent to panic. Fortunately, this format has an obscene number of token makers.
- **Resistance Is Futile** — A two-mana removal spell sounds great, even at sorcery speed. The catch is that this only hits nonartifact creatures, which is a pretty ugly restriction in a format overflowing with Borg and Spacecraft.
- **Rogue Artificial Intelligence** — Basic landcycling is a terrific safety valve in Limited, especially if it lets you fix your colors or attempt another wildly irresponsible splash. Rogue Artificial Intelligence gives you that option without saddling you with a useless creature when you draw it late.
- **Rok-Tahk, Budding Scientist** — This is super omega ramp, which sounds fantastic until you remember you’ve already spent six mana casting Rok-Tahk. In Limited, how many gigantic spells are you actually going to have left to cast? This isn’t Commander, where every third card is a seven-drop and a questionable financial decision.
- **Romulan Bird-of-Prey** — For such a small mana investment, this has a surprising amount of upside, even if it takes a while to get the engines running. You have two ways to power it up, so you can use whichever fits your board instead of jumping through one specific hoop.
- **Rules of Acquisition** — I love me some Control Magic action. There’s just something deeply satisfying about taking your opponent’s best creature and using it to beat them over the head. Unfortunately, giving up three cards in the process is an absolutely massive price to pay.
- **Rutherford, Upgraded Ensign** — This has a pretty good chance of finding a home in multiple archetypes, which is exactly what I like to see from a solid Limited card. Spacecraft decks are naturally going to have a bunch of artifacts running around, and the Borg went all-in on the artifact lifestyle.
- **Saavik, Stoic Student** — I really wish this was a little cheaper because five mana is an awful lot to ask for what you’re getting here. It also has the unfortunate problem of telegraphing your intentions pretty loudly when you pass the turn without playing anything.
- **Salt Vampire** — This is going to leave more than a few opponents feeling pretty salty. Taking your opponent’s best card is an incredibly powerful effect, even if you’re giving them a replacement card in the process.
- **Sarcophagus, Ship of the Dead** — Station ten looks absurd until you remember that this boosts all your creatures’ power. That makes getting the ship online easier than the number suggests. The team-wide boost also helps push damage through right away, and eventually you get a sizable threat of your own.
- **Sarek, Federation Ambassador** — There are reasonable games where Sarek draws you an extra card every turn. That’s genuinely good, even if you’re paying the price of a three-color mana base. In the occasional magical Christmasland game with enough creature types, he might even draw two cards a turn and snowball out of control.
- **Saru, Unthreatened** — Some games Saru will feel like the best card in your deck. Your opponent aims premium removal at a creature, you respond with Saru, and their carefully planned turn falls apart. Those blowouts are why Limited is fun. Well, for one player.
- **Saurian Explorer** — Attacking as a 6/5 for four mana is already a lot of lizard coming at your opponent. Activating this to put down an extra land and turn it into an 8/7 is even better. That extra land also triggers your other landfall effects, giving the ability value beyond making an already huge attacker bigger.
- **Savvy Profiteer** — I’m tempted to bump this to a 3.0, but that depends on how fast the format ends up being. A two-mana ramp creature that can also attack for two is a promising start. Treasure tokens do more than fix your mana here.
- **Scotty, Miracle Worker** — I wonder if Scotty realizes just how absurd he would be if he weren’t restricted to doing his thing during your main phase. I’m pretty sure Scotty doesn’t know. Obviously, you’re going to need some spells worth effectively flashing back and creatures with enters-the-battlefield abilities worth reusing.
- **Sela, Tal Shiar Paradox** — This actually has a nice little interaction with Khan, Vengeful Heart. Being able to exile him for some extra value and then cast him again is exactly the kind of nonsense I enjoy finding in Limited. We’re still talking about a Hill Giant, and those aren’t famous for surviving combat in 2026.
- **Set Phasers to . . .** — Set phasers to MID. Pros: It only costs one mana, and being a modal spell means it can trigger some additional benefits from cards that care about those sorts of things. Cons: It doesn’t actually do a whole lot. That’s pretty much the entire review right there, but I’m contractually obligated to keep typing.
- **Seven of Nine** — The usual disclaimer applies: grades are for an average deck of these colors, not an imaginary deck where every synergy piece appears on schedule. Fortunately for Seven of Nine, there are a LOT of Face a Dilemma cards here.
- **Seven of Nine, Unassimilated** — I’m grading Seven without assuming either differently named lands ability will turn on. Sure, there will be games where the five-color soup monstrosities I keep recommending unlock everything. In your average Limited deck, though, those abilities are bonuses, not something you should count on.
- **Shields Up!** — I’m always a sucker for these cheap tricks that grant hexproof or indestructible. There’s just something deeply satisfying about watching your opponent spend their premium removal spell only for you to ruin their entire day with a single mana.
- **Shuttle Ace** — A 4/4 for five mana that occasionally remembers it can fly and gains you a little life whenever it gets tapped. At least the life gain has some nice synergy with station, giving you a little extra value for helping get your Spacecraft up and running. That’s certainly something, even if it’s not particularly exciting.
- **Sickbay** — This card can get absolutely sick. Unfortunately, the treatment isn’t cheap, and I’m not entirely sure my insurance is going to cover it. I appreciate that you still get a counter even if you haven’t gained life.
- **Silicate Surveyor** — You only get so many expensive slots in a Limited deck, and the basic landcyclers already have their eyes on them. Silicate Surveyor at least replaces itself, which makes the investment easier to justify. It’s not spectacular, but I won’t complain about getting a card back with my creature.
- **Spatial Anomaly** — I’m slotting this directly between Artificial Anomaly and Gaseous Anomaly. Picking off a cheap creature is more valuable than draining two life, and less valuable than drawing two cards. Groundbreaking analysis, I know. Somebody get me a research grant. Removal stapled to a land is useful when the target lines up.
- **Species 8472** — Gaining three life is already enough to make this a reasonable Limited playable, especially when you’re getting a respectable body along with it. That’s a nice way to help stabilize the battlefield against an aggressive opponent. Of course, three life is just the starting point.
- **Spock, Always Your Friend** — It’s only logical that Spock is always my friend, especially when I’m drafting Izzet. In a deck stuffed with noncreature spells, you’re going to hit off this ability with remarkable consistency. Honestly, it feels more inevitable than Thanos.
- **Stable Wormhole** — Curve out with a two-drop into Stable Wormhole on turn three and you might put your opponent so far behind that they spend the next several turns trying to catch up. Draw it late, however, and it might as well be blank cardboard.
- **Starfleet Academy** — There is no universe in which I’m putting this in a Limited deck. Even if you’re all-in on different creature types, you need seven of them on the battlefield just to come out one mana ahead. SEVEN.
- **Starfleet Security** — I’d like this a lot more if the death trigger worked on your opponent’s turn. Requiring it to die on yours is an awkward restriction for something that’s supposed to generate value. Attack for one and your opponent can just take it. They’re not obligated to block and hand you a trigger.
- **Starfleet Spanner Jockey** — There are an awful lot of random 2/2s running around this format, and this jockey is perfectly happy to keep them in check. Sometimes all you need is a creature that makes your opponent think twice before turning their little dudes sideways. That’s useful when you’re stabilizing early or trying to win through the air.
- **Storied Veteran** — This seems like a pretty solid card if you’re playing a hyper-aggressive deck or have a bunch of sacrifice outlets looking for something to throw into the dumpster. In either of those situations, there’s some legitimate value here. The inability to block is a brutal drawback, though.
- **Stray from History** — This grade is primarily for the first copy because the value of this card drops off pretty dramatically from there. I could certainly see myself playing two in the right deck, but I’m struggling to imagine many situations where I’d actually want a third. Choosing a mode should be straightforward.
- **Subcommander T'Pol** — Countering an activated or triggered ability will occasionally ruin someone’s day, but most games this is going to be a Frost Lynx with flash. That extra option matters even when you don’t choose it: you still face a dilemma and collect any payoffs you’ve drafted.
- **Subspace Anomaly** — In retrospect, maybe I should have put all the Anomalies in one section. There’s only so many times I can explain that colorless lands have a cost before I start repeating myself. Anyway, here we go again. Two 1/1 tokens are fine. They can chump block, become sacrifice fodder, or help station a Spacecraft.
- **Sulu, Space Swashbuckler** — Sulu is a perfectly serviceable body on his own, but we all know that serviceable doesn’t pay the bills in modern Limited. Fortunately, he’s got a little more going for him than just respectable stats.
- **Support Mission** — This is a bit like Glorious Anthem, except the bonus only applies to creatures you play after it hits the battlefield. That’s a pretty enormous “except.” Glorious Anthem can immediately turn an existing army into a threat.
- **Suurok-Class Science Vessel** — I do love me some creatures that replace themselves when they enter the battlefield. Getting a body while keeping the cards flowing is one of those things that never really goes out of style in Limited.
- **Suus Mahna Practitioner** — Tappers have bounced in and out of Limited relevance for years. Lately they’ve had a rough time of it: faster games and stronger creatures leave less spare mana to spend locking things down. It also doesn’t help that this costs three mana when, historically, we were getting perfectly respectable tappers for two.
- **Sylvia Tilly, Joy of Discovery** — This is obviously a significant step down from Siege Veteran, but let’s be fair. Siege Veteran was a pretty busted Limited card, so that’s not exactly a devastating criticism.
- **Syndicate Liquidators** — This can snowball in a hurry on normal land drops alone. Add anything that puts extra lands onto the battlefield, and your Liquidators start getting terrifying.
- **Tactical Officer** — If you thought Grizzly Bears were unimpressive in modern Limited, allow me to introduce you to the exciting world of Grizzly Bears with only one toughness. Apparently, Starfleet has been making some serious budget cuts to its tactical division.
- **Tal Shiar Analyst** — This is an interesting option for Izzet spells: a more expensive Thermo-Alchemist, but with a body that can actually attack. When the way is clear, get in for three and then untap it with noncreature spells to keep pinging away. That’s a lot of potential damage from one card.
- **Tal Shiar Operative** — This is a pretty solid card if you’re planning on being aggressive or have enough Face a Dilemma payoffs to take advantage of it. In those decks, it can put in some respectable work without requiring you to jump through too many hoops. On defense, however, it’s miserable.
- **Targ Wrangler** — In an average Limited deck, Targ Wrangler isn’t doing much to earn a slot. Put it in a hyper-aggressive deck with a steady supply of 1/1 tokens to throw around, though, and it can become a genuine threat. This is one of those cards where the surrounding thirty nine matter more than the card itself.
- **Tasha Yar, Out of Time** — A two-drop that can replace itself is a great start. There’s some risk that Tasha trades off early and finds something you can’t afford to cast yet, but that’s a manageable downside for two mana. She can also come back and do it again.
- **Tea, Earl Grey, Hot** — At least this replaces itself, so the floor is decent. I’m going to have to duck to avoid the ceiling, though. I expect Tea, Earl Grey, Hot to show up mostly in Izzet spells. It’s a cheap cantrip that gives you a little extra utility while keeping the spells flowing.
- **Tell Me What You See** — I’ll tell you what I see: a card that might be much better than it looks. Or a steaming pile of garbage that makes me look like an idiot for giving it a 2.5. That’s part of the fun. The key here is putting this in an aggressive deck with plenty of ways to face a dilemma.
- **Temporal Anomaly** — The final Anomaly has arrived. Thank God. I was beginning to think these things were reproducing faster than Tribbles. It’s another colorless land offering a decent late-game effect that doesn’t quite justify the stress it puts on your mana base. If the rest of your lands are excellent, sure, you can afford the luxury.
- **Temporal Conqueror** — Remember Eccentric Farmer from Innistrad: Midnight Hunt? That humble common overperformed by a mile, and Temporal Conqueror looks like an upgrade. You get the same graveyard value, except that missing a land now gives you a Treasure. That’s a useful consolation prize rather than a complete whiff.
- **Tenacious Drone** — It doesn’t quite replace itself with a full-sized clone, but getting a 1/1 token is still a decent deal. An extra artifact creature can block, feed a sacrifice outlet, or support your other Borg synergies.
- **Test of Conviction** — Here’s another modal card where neither mode is particularly impressive. There are certainly going to be go-wide decks that love the +1/+1 counter half of this, especially when they’re looking to turn a board full of little dudes into something considerably more threatening.
- **Test of Courage** — You just shouldn’t play this card. Seriously, put it down. Not even a pile of Face a Dilemma payoffs can rescue two extremely situational modes with no way to replace the card when both are dead. Some narrow modal spells at least offer a cantrip. This one leaves you staring at a blank.
- **Test of Identity** — This has two obvious homes: aggressive Klingons and Izzet spells. Klingons want another way to push damage; Izzet wants a noncreature spell that contributes to its various payoffs. Same card, different job descriptions. It’s still playable elsewhere, just less efficient without those synergies.
- **Test of Loyalty** — The only thing this card is testing is my patience if you try to jam it into a deck without multiple Face a Dilemma payoffs. We’ve already seen the danger of filling a deck with underwhelming spells just because they trigger a mechanic. Test of Loyalty isn’t getting a pass.
- **Test of Priorities** — The Face a Dilemma trigger is welcome, but Test of Priorities still does useful work when you don’t have any payoffs on the battlefield. You can save a creature from removal or reuse an enters-the-battlefield trigger. That’s the kind of utility I want from a Beam Me Up effect.
- **The Caretaker** — The Caretaker needs a little time before the ridiculous stuff starts happening. Playing it and a modal spell in the same turn isn’t realistic on curve, so your opponent will usually get a chance to respond before you start having fun.
- **The City on the Edge of Forever** — Turning this into an actual sweeper takes a suspiciously specific sequence of events. You need to produce another creature token before the third chapter and keep your fragile little 0/1 alive until everything comes together. That’s a lot of faith to put in something that could lose a fight with a stiff breeze.
- **The Destruction of Praxis** — Yes, I know this looks low for a sweeper. The problem is that spending five mana to blow up the battlefield isn’t nearly as exciting when half your opponent’s creatures might survive.
- **The Doctor, EMH** — Five total power and toughness for five mana is a reasonable starting point. Being able to distribute two of those +1/+1 counters is a nice bonus, especially when you’ve got an evasive creature or something that just needs a little extra muscle. In Selesnya, the ceiling gets much higher.
- **The Duras Sisters** — This might seem a little high at first glance, but I think people are going to underestimate the amount of work these two can do. You start with slightly above-rate stats, and when the original creature dies, you get another body with two power and haste.
- **The Holodeck** — First, yes, you still have to pay mana for the cards you play with this. If everything were free, the Commander Rules Committee would be having a collective conniption fit before the set even released. The effect is powerful, but seven mana is a serious opening bid.
- **The Inner Light** — I’m typically happy to pay five mana for a Reanimate effect if there’s something extra included. This is like one of those fantasy football trades where the other manager keeps adding players until the offer becomes too ridiculous to decline. First, you get your creature back.
- **The Measure of a Man** — This would be absolutely busted if Beam Me Up could return enchantments. We already saw how ridiculous that kind of interaction could get with The Princess Takes Flight back in Wilds of Eldraine. Unfortunately, that’s not an option here, which takes away a massive chunk of the potential value.
- **The Naked Time** — I’m going to show some remarkable restraint and avoid commenting on how unserious this card is or the obnoxious jokes its name is inevitably going to generate. The card is bad enough that, with any luck, we’ll hardly have to hear the jokes.
- **The Needs of the Many** — Personally, I’m taking a hard pass on this one. I’m sure there will be the occasional spectacular blowout where everything lines up perfectly and you look like a genius for putting this in your deck.
- **The Traveler** — The Traveler is mostly a pile of stats, but sometimes that’s exactly what you need. Vigilance and reach are a particularly nice combination. You can get in for damage without tapping him, and your opponent still has to worry about their flyers getting swatted out of the sky on the way back.
- **The Wrath of Khan** — I’d happily play The Wrath of Khan for its first ability alone. Everything after that is just gravy on a card that’s already doing its job. Rummaging away a card while making your opponent sacrifice a nontoken permanent is particularly nasty in a format filled with Officer tokens.
- **There's Coffee in that Nebula** — I’m somehow going to restrain myself from making the approximately 47 obvious jokes about this card’s name, which is particularly impressive considering I’m sitting here downing coffee after coffee just to get these reviews written. At this point, I’m fairly certain my blood type is espresso.
- **Those Old Scientists** — I initially misread this card and thought it was decent, then I realized you discarded two cards in step one. Basically it’s a slow and limited Regrowth with some card selection tossed in and a bit of messing with the graveyard. I’m not sure what those scientists are up to, but it’s certainly nothing good.
- **Transporter Malfunction** — That poor redshirt has seen better days. Although, considering what Janeway did to Tuvix, I’m starting to think Starfleet should stop letting people near transporters. Lightning Strike has always been excellent in Limited. Sorcery speed hurts, but three damage for two mana still handles plenty of early threats.
- **Tricorder** — Oh, no. I’m not falling for this one again. I remember S.H.I.E.L.D. Spy Kit looking like a low-end playable before it tore through an entire format. To be fair, Spy Kit gave pseudo-vigilance, which was a huge part of its success.
- **Troubling Tribbles** — Flavor-wise, this is an easy 5.0. Playability-wise? Well, let’s just say the Tribbles are causing trouble for all the wrong reasons. The inability to block is an absolutely massive downside. In your average Limited deck, this is borderline unplayable garbage.
- **Type-2 Phaser** — I know at least five Magic boomers who saw this and immediately thought, ‘Standard Phaser.’ Most of you weren’t around in the olden days, but trust me, that joke absolutely crushed with a demographic of a certain age. Unfortunately, the card is less impressive than my nostalgia.
- **U.S.S. Cerritos, Following Up** — This is entirely a question of how quickly your deck can station a Spacecraft. If you’ve built to get ships online ahead of schedule, a 4/4 flyer is a reward worth considering.
- **U.S.S. Discovery, at Black Alert** — Discover is an absolutely filthy mechanic: card advantage and mana advantage wrapped together. Sure, there’s randomness, but if the spell isn’t right for the moment, you can simply put it into your hand. Discovery does that when it enters the battlefield, then threatens to repeat it every time it attacks.
- **U.S.S. Enterprise-A, Free Spirit** — I’m not exactly surprised that the U.S.S. Enterprise is a fantastic Limited card. It would be pretty weird to make one of the most iconic ships in science fiction history into an unplayable pile of garbage.
- **U.S.S. Enterprise-D, Galaxy-Class** — A card advantage engine that eventually becomes a big flyer with vigilance for only three mana? Yeah, sign me up. This is what separates the Enterprise-D from a lot of other Spacecraft. Many ships sit around doing their best impression of expensive paperweights until you’ve finished stationing them.
- **U.S.S. Excelsior, Cutting Edge** — Four mana is a little too much to pay to destroy a tapped creature at sorcery speed. Eight station counters is also a bit too much work for a 4/4 flyer. But throw those two slightly underwhelming effects together, and suddenly you’ve got a perfectly respectable Limited card.
- **U.S.S. Reliant, From Hell's Heart** — I want a four-drop waiting in my graveyard before I cast this. Getting something back immediately is the reliable part of the card; the Spacecraft itself takes quite a bit more work. Station ten. TEN! That’s an outrageous number of counters.
- **U.S.S. Voyager, Lone Wanderer** — We’ve come a long way from the days when Explosive Vegetation was somehow a Constructed staple. These days, spending that much mana to ramp isn’t nearly as impressive, especially when you don’t even get to choose which lands you’re getting.
- **Una Chin-Riley, Number One** — Oh look, another five-drop that doesn’t actually do anything when it enters the battlefield. That’s certainly becoming a recurring theme, and not one I’m particularly excited about. The body is perfectly respectable and provides a pretty significant chunk of the eight power you need to get things going.
- **Unexplored Territory** — I like that they used this version instead of Evolving Wilds, even though you can argue it’s the worse card. In this particular format, the difference actually matters. Both can find the colored source you’re missing.
- **V'Ger, the Intruder** — Seven mana might as well be seven light-years away in some Limited games, but sometimes you just need a big, goofy top-end creature to finish things off. If you haven’t managed to pick up anything better, V’Ger is a perfectly reasonable option.
- **Vash, Freelance Archaeologist** — We all know Golgarbage loves rummaging through its graveyard like a raccoon digging through a dumpster. Vash has apparently figured out how to make a living from it. Every time you start messing with your graveyard, she rewards you with an actual, factual Magic card. Not a token or some temporary consolation prize.
- **Vengeance Mission** — Are you following this up with a sweeper? No? Well, then this is probably going to be a pretty sad excuse for a mission of vengeance. You’ll still be able to take them down fairly quick through trading in combat, but this will be at its best if you have sacrifice synergies.
- **Vexing Drone** — This is pretty much the definition of curve filler. You’re not playing it because you’re excited about what it does. You’re playing it because you somehow made it through the draft without enough early creatures and now you’ve got a problem to solve.
- **Vulcan Mind-Meld** — Noooopppppeeee. This isn’t even the card advantage it might look like at first glance. You’re spending a card to get two of your opponent’s while handing them one of yours in the process.
- **Warp Core** — The floor is perfectly reasonable. Warp Core fixes your mana while replacing itself, which is a decent deal when your mana base looks like it was assembled by somebody who considers basic lands a personal insult. Some decks will play it for that alone.
- **Warp Speed** — You pretty much need to be playing blue before I’d consider this. That’s a significant strike against a green card in your average Limited deck. In five-color Strange New Worlds soup, though, the differently named lands make it far more appealing.
- **Wesley Crusher, Starfleet Cadet** — I’m not a player, I just crush a lot. Wesley gives me an excuse to make dated musical references that a significant portion of Magic players have probably never heard of. Fortunately, he can also deliver some soul-crushing plays of his own.
- **Will Riker, First in the Field** — Riker offers temporary ramp that can also trigger landfall multiple times. In a deck with the right payoffs, that’s a useful combination. Pulling lands out of your deck slightly improves your future draws too, though the effect is much smaller than people tend to think. The body is the letdown.
- **Worf, Honor-Bound Warrior** — For a Klingon who’s constantly talking about glorious battle, Worf seems awfully dependent on everyone else showing up to the fight first. I’m not going to lie: I expected more from him. This feels like a card that could have cost one mana without causing the collapse of Limited as we know it.
- **Worf, Intelligence Contractor** — Worf can turn a good attacking position into an absolute nightmare for your opponent. That’s exactly the kind of pressure a creature-heavy red deck wants. Sacrifice synergies make the exchange even nastier, letting you squeeze value out of creatures that were already helping you press the advantage.
- **Xenobotanist** — I usually love a creature that makes all five colors of mana. It’s an excellent enabler for irresponsible splashes, and at least it has a body to block with. The awkward part is that the multicolor decks in this format care about differently named lands.
- **Xindi Surveyors** — Starting out as a 3/2 instead of the usual single point of toughness these type of landfall creatures normally get is a pretty significant upgrade. That extra toughness makes a world of difference when you’re trying to attack through the various 1/1 tokens running around this format.
- **Year of Hell** — Hell is a draft where I have to play this card even once, let alone for an entire year. Someone looked at Secret Rendezvous, a card that was already unplayable garbage, and apparently asked, “What if we made it worse?” And somehow, they succeeded.
- **Zefram Cochrane, Warp Pioneer** — Cochrane is close to unplayable in a generic Limited deck. Put him in a dedicated Azorius Spacecraft build, though, and suddenly he has an actual job. When you’re trying to station a fleet, having another piece to speed things along earns its keep. Outside that strategy, there’s not enough here to justify a slot.
- **Zero, Incorporeal Sensate** — With Zero, I’d generally target something expensive and treat the ability as a sort of pseudo-discard spell. Your opponent technically still has the card, but if the tax keeps them from casting it for several turns, you’ve accomplished something meaningful.
