# SOS — Rough Drafts notes

> Source: **Rough Drafts** (Samp / Sam Pratt) — 9 Secrets of Strixhaven episodes (57–65).
> Distilled 2026-08-23 from YouTube auto-caption transcripts; card names corrected against the
> printed SOS list, uncertain readings marked `(?)`.
>
> This channel is not a set review. Episodes titled after a single card are **format-theory essays
> that use the card as a lens** — of the six card-titled SOS episodes, only one (ep 58, Rubble
> Rouser) actually sustains its namesake for most of its runtime. The value here is the argument
> about how the format works, and the host's **stated confidence**, which is preserved below rather
> than flattened into grades. Samp explicitly frames the show as a complement to full card-by-card
> reviews, not a replacement.

## Supersessions (newer take wins)

- **"Don't view the colleges as monolithic" — his own opening thesis, largely reversed.** Ep 57
  spends its whole preamble arguing that each college has several distinct builds and that you
  should chase the power in front of you rather than the prescribed archetype → **Ep 65: the sheer
  density of gold cards defeated that.** *"If you are in the right lane and you're getting
  absolutely hooked up… you just get all the cards you want and you just build the same deck every
  time, which is like the highest quality version of the deck."* He explicitly blames the all-gold,
  no-hybrid design and says fewer gold cards would have produced the variation he wanted.
  **Verdict: find the open college; the build then writes itself.**
- **Silverquill is the best college → Lorehold is.** Ep 57: *"I don't think that's going to be a huge
  problem… I do think this is going to be the strongest college like in the data just in the
  aggregate."* → **Ep 65: Lorehold was the best deck and it was a surprise** — *"I don't think a lot
  of people clocked Lorehold as the best deck."* Silverquill stays strong and consistent; it is not
  the top deck.
- **Blue-green tempo is not a real deck → it is.** Ep 59: *"I don't think that's possible… I'm not a
  believer in Cuboid Colony green blue beatdown."* → **Ep 62: Samp opens by conceding it** — *"I
  famously said that blue green tempo was not a thing 2 weeks ago"* — after TheRosemaryTea
  demonstrated **blue-green small** and he trophied with it himself. **Verdict: real, narrow, and
  matchup-polarised (eats soup, loses to Silverquill).**
- **Control is a pillar (build it) → control is a pillar (but don't play it).** Eps 60–61 build the
  multicolor and control decks enthusiastically → **Ep 64: he stopped drafting them entirely** — he
  *"just really did not enjoy drafting or playing the control decks… and I also wasn't really doing
  very well with these decks"* — pivoted hard into creature decks and started winning more →
  **Ep 65 stands by it.** Note the nuance: he never retracts the structural claim that control is
  consistently viable in SOS, only his own fit with it.
- **Witherbloom "at risk of being a flop" → confirmed worst, and permanently over-drafted.** Ep 57
  hedged that BG was *"the college that is most at risk of being a flop, of being misunderstood"* →
  Ep 59 shows the three highest-picked gold commons in the format are all BG and argues you should
  build the deck *"like you are not going to get any copies of Bogwater Lumaret"* → **Ep 65: worst
  archetype, and still over-drafted at the end of the format** — *"even to this day, you could
  probably just still ignore black green and have a very good win rate."*
- **Converge looked like a joke → it was very good.** Ep 65: cards like Archaic's Agony and Arcane
  Omens *"just kind of looked like what are we doing here… and they ended up being very, very good."*
  He ties the miss directly to under-rating **Potioner's Trove** — had the Trove been graded in the
  B range up front, the converge cards would have been rated higher too.
- **Pursue the Past: community C → settled B.** Ep 58 already flags it as *"doing really well in the
  early data, and I don't think it's a fluke"* → **Ep 65: *"one of the top commons, possibly number
  two behind Elite Interceptor."*** He calls the two-mana front end (vs. the three-mana Tormenting
  Voice variants in MID and FIN) the reason.
- **Elite Interceptor: predicted a clear notch below the Inspectors → basically level.** Ep 57:
  *"I think this will be among the best commons. I think it has a shot at being the best common, um
  but I think it will be in contention rather than a clear best common like the inspectors."* →
  **Ep 65: it posted roughly the same win rate as Novice Inspector in MKM** — *"I did not expect it
  to be that high."*
- **Flashback under-rated by everyone, himself included.** Ep 65: *"I think in general we kind of
  underrated how good flashback is… we should probably just like bump up our estimation of flashback
  for future sets."* Eight-mana flashback on Molten Note was genuinely relevant.
- **Rares belong in the pre-release conversation.** Ep 65: the community clocked that white had four
  strong non-mythic rares, but missed that the control decks had a **critical mass of go-over-the-top
  win conditions**. He commits to covering rares in future Hot Topics episodes.

## Format speed / meta read

**The thesis: SOS is a mana-rich, gold-saturated guild set that goes over the top, and the whole
draft is about finding the one open college — because once you are alone in it, the format hands you
the maximum-quality version of that deck automatically.**

- **Five colleges, all-gold, no hybrid.** Silverquill (WB) · Prismari (UR) · Witherbloom (BG) ·
  Lorehold (WR) · Quandrix (UG). Per college: three gold commons, seven gold uncommons, four-to-five
  rares, one-to-two mythics, plus a bonus-sheet rare. These are **true gold cards** — you must
  produce both colours — which is why off-supported pairs are not a plan and why finding the open
  lane is so disproportionately rewarded. Ep 65 names this as the format's central design flaw:
  Shards of Alara's hybrid density gave you more usable cards mid-draft; SOS gives you a binary.
- **The format is mana-hungry and the mana is unusually good.** You get **both** a cycle of common
  surveil duals (in ~50% of packs) **and** Terramorphic Expanse — normally a set gets one or the
  other — plus treasures in red, basic-land fetching in green, and Potioner's Trove. Ep 58's
  practical read: *"just having extra mana in this format, I found is really really important"* —
  flashback and prepared spells mean you essentially never run out of things to do with mana, so raw
  mana output over the course of a game converts to wins even without a specific curve skip.
- **Control is a genuine pillar — rare in Limited.** Ep 61's framing is deliberately blunt: *"Control
  is good in Secrets of Strixhaven because there is a good control deck in Secrets of Strixhaven."*
  Not because the format is slow — he rejects average-game-length as a metric outright — but because
  the tools exist: cheap red interaction, real counterspells, mana accelerants, and a **critical
  mass of rare "I win" buttons** (Mathemagics, Exhibition Tidecaller, Crackle with Power, Traumatic
  Critique, Together as One, the paradigm spells/Practicums). Since play boosters put a given rare on
  the table roughly every other draft, the deck comes together consistently.
- **The two-toughness and four-toughness breakpoints define combat.** Ep 58: four toughness blocks
  every unmodified two-drop in the game and dodges Last Gasp, so creatures up the curve with four
  toughness trade at a mana advantage. Conversely, Tome Blast and Burst Lightning both punish two
  toughness — which is exactly why Cost of Brilliance's +1/+1 counter matters so much in the small-
  game decks.
- **"Small game" is the operative concept in the black-based decks.** Ep 59: keep the battlefield
  small with heavy removal (he wants **six, sometimes seven or eight** pieces of interaction), so
  that incidental advantages — a counter, a drain, a pest — actually decide the board. This applies
  to Silverquill and to the good build of Witherbloom alike.
- **Multicolor is not "soup," and Samp bans the word.** Ep 60 splits multicolor into three buckets:
  (1) **base-green midrange** — heavy green, cheap green fixing, off-colour basics, proactive curve
  with converge top-end; (2) **base-blue control** — almost always UR, leaning on dual lands,
  treasures and Potioner's Trove rather than basics, splashing white interaction when the mana is
  free; (3) **"piles"** — theorycrafted base-white control built off the strong white rares, which he
  never actually saw in the format. Crucially, **SOS does not reward the classic soup plan of hoarding
  fixing to catch late rares**, because the best rares (Practiced Offense, Stirring Hopesinger,
  Informed Inkwright, Hardened Academic, Colorstorm Stallion, Moseo, Postmortem Professor) all want a
  dedicated base deck, not a splash.
- **Repartee is the deck-building constraint worth respecting.** Ep 65 names it the format's one real
  density puzzle — *"the deck building challenge of getting enough ways to target creatures"* — and
  says he'd like to see it return, preferring it to heroic/valiant because of how open-ended the
  qualifying spells are.
- **Prepared rewards trading your creatures off, not hoarding them.** Ep 57 draws the explicit
  analogy to spacecraft in Edge of Eternities: secure the board and keep your life total high first,
  because that is what buys you the option to hold a valuable prepared creature later. People will be
  too precious with these.
- **Best-of-three is where this format is good; best-of-one distorts it.** Ep 65 is emphatic: the
  format is *"pretty middle-of-the-road, below average"* overall and a *"below average set for the
  last couple years"*, but he *"had a blast"* in best-of-three because the open-lane decks produce
  huge playable counts and therefore real sideboard options, and because control's existence makes
  main-deck removal counts genuinely hard to guess in game one.
- **Where the format's read comes from matters.** Arena best-of-one is the default UI path, which
  floods those queues with players who take Bogwater Lumaret and Pest Mascot on sight. That single
  fact keeps Witherbloom over-drafted all format and, in his view, colours the public perception of
  SOS more than the set itself does.

## Archetypes

Settled ranking from the Retrospective, with the mid-format detail underneath.

1. **Lorehold (WR) — the best deck. Card-quality midrange.** *"I do think it was the best deck in the
   format"* — and it wins by playing individually good cards up the curve and taking whichever role
   the matchup calls for, not by assembling the leaves-the-graveyard synergy. The leaves-the-graveyard
   theme is a **sliding scale you can ignore**: Ep 65 praises the design specifically because *"the
   deck was very good even if you didn't have anything that cared about that mechanic."* The default
   build plays slightly "stompily" — big bodies, Rubble Rouser mana, burn as reach. Genuinely
   aggressive Lorehold builds exist and are successful, and they are the ones that don't want Rubble
   Rouser or Potioner's Trove.
2. **Silverquill (WB) — proactive midrange-to-aggro, the most consistent college.** Ep 57 predicted
   it would top the aggregate because *"if you just mush all of its gold cards together, you get a
   pretty good deck"*, and flagged it as the college with the least nuance. In practice it is a
   **Cost of Brilliance small-game deck** with a lot of interaction, repartee triggers off cheap
   spells, and reach from drain effects — not a pure curve-out aggro deck. It is the worst matchup
   for blue-green small, because counterspells don't stop repartee triggers.
3. **Prismari (UR) — the control pillar, plus two other builds.** Three distinct shells: (a) the
   **draw-go UR spells control** deck built around Wisdom of Ages, wanting a very high density of
   cheap interaction and deliberately **few creatures**; (b) the **tap-out / converge** build that is
   happier with Divergent Equation, plays cheap accelerants and splashes; (c) a **Prismari midrange
   flyers deck** (ep 64) that Samp settled into late — Elemental Mascot, Campus Composer, Spectacular
   Skywhale, Visionary's Dance — which un-taxes cards like Thunderdrum Soloist and Stadium Tidalmage
   by giving the opponent's removal other targets. There is also a **blue-red small** prowess/burst
   build (Tackle Artist, Expressive Firedancer, Deluge Virtuoso) that both Samp and the guest rate as
   the weaker sibling of blue-green small, because the burn is dead against the big-mana decks.
4. **Quandrix (UG) — three separate decks under one banner.** (a) **Green midrange multicolor**: heavy
   base green, Studious First-Year / Environmental Scientist / Strixhaven Skycoach fixing, converge
   top-end, off-colour basics — the Christopher Larson Pro Tour deck is the model. (b) **Blue-green
   small** (guest, ep 62): a lean one-two-three curve, a definitional tempo deck built on
   counterspells and protection, whose *"threats are not good"* and whose best common threat is
   Cuboid Colony **specifically for flash**. It eats the multicolor decks and loses badly to
   Silverquill. Do not commit to it from pick one — TheRosemaryTea's advice is to start considering
   it *"halfway through pack one."* (c) A rare **X-spells** build off Geometer's Arthropod, which she
   describes as coming together only rarely.
5. **Witherbloom (BG) — the worst college, and the trap.** The lifegain/infusion build is the one the
   field drafts and it is *"so easy to pick these decks apart."* The build that works is a **black
   proactive small-game deck** — Ep 59's central claim is that it is *"more of a black deck than a
   green deck"* and closer to a sister of Silverquill than to the prescribed BG. Build it as though
   you will get **zero** copies of Bogwater Lumaret; the incidental lifegain then pads you against
   aggro and turns on Thornfist Striker anyway. You generally want a strong BG rare (Cauldron of
   Essence, Moseo, Postmortem Professor) as the reason to be here. A separate **Cauldron of Essence
   grindy discard** build (ep 64) is the other legitimate home, and it wants the opposite of the
   beatdown deck.

**"Piles"** — base-white control double-splashing off the strong white rares (Moment of Reckoning,
Antiquities on the Loose, Professor Dellian Fel, Together as One), leaning on white interaction and
Diary of Dreams for card flow. Pure theorycraft: he never saw one all format and says so.

## Card tips

Hedges are the host's own; where he is uncertain, so is the note.

### White
- **Elite Interceptor** — one mana 1/2, enters prepared with Rejoinder. Ep 57's careful read: the
  tension is real (you must sink three full mana into it before trading it off, unlike Thraben
  Inspector's detachable clue) and he expected *"a big notch worse than Novice Inspector."* **Ep 65:
  it landed roughly level with Novice Inspector, largely because Rejoinder is an on-demand repartee
  trigger in Silverquill.** Probably the best common in the set.
- **Practiced Offense** — *"the best rare in the set"* and *"obviously like way too good for this
  limited format."* He hated playing against it. Pack-one-pick-one, and it will pull you into white
  creature decks to support it.
- **Stirring Hopesinger** — three-mana 1/3 that pumps the team on repartee. One of the four white
  rares he names as the reason white is deep; a real reason to draft a white aggressive deck.
- **Informed Inkwright** — two-mana 2/2 making a flyer on every repartee trigger. Named by the guest
  as blue-green small's *"worst nightmare"* — a snowballing two-drop it cannot answer.
- **Antiquities on the Loose** — makes two spirits and has flashback; the fourth of the strong white
  rares and a go-wide payoff.
- **Dig Site Inventory** — one mana, +1/+1 counter and vigilance, flashback for one. Called *"a fiddly
  little glue piece"* that keeps recurring: two cheap repartee triggers in Silverquill, two
  leaves-the-graveyard triggers in Lorehold, and a way to grow flyers. Actively looked for alongside
  Conciliator's Duelist.
- **Ajani's Response** — five mana, three less against a tapped creature. *"A really good splash if
  your mana is like free"*; one of the off-colour cards over-performing in UR decks.
- **Repel Calamity** — bonus-sheet white removal; also flagged as over-performing in blue-red. Note the
  guest's caveat: it is often dead against Silverquill's small creatures but live against tall
  green/Quandrix bodies.
- **Owlin Historian** — three-mana 2/3 flyer that became *"the backbone of my Lorehold midrange and
  Silverquill decks"* once he leaned into flyers. Pairs with Dig Site Inventory and Render Speechless.
- **Interjection** — one-mana +2/+2 first strike. He expected not to play trick-style cards and was
  *"pretty happy with this card"* — protecting a threat or winning a double block comes up. Also a
  cheap repartee trigger for a Conciliator's Duelist deck.
- **Rapier Wit** — cantripping repartee trigger. Goes *"way up in value"* against decks stabilising
  behind one or two big creatures; a sideboard card for Silverquill decks short on targeting spells.
- **Harsh Annotation** — two-mana removal that gives them an Inkling. Explicit warning: *"I do not
  think you should put this in your deck almost ever"* — playable only if you are simultaneously very
  low on interaction **and** very low on repartee triggers. Aggregate *"D-minus, maybe not F."*
- **Quill-Blade Laureate** — two-mana 1/1 double striker, prepared spell grants double strike. *"Very
  very potent with Tackle Artist"* in the low-power aggressive shells.
- **Honorbound Page** — explicitly *"another very undesirable card. Not one I'm putting in very many
  decks"*, but its value rises in the cheese/pump packages.
- **Eager Glyphmage** — four-mana 3/3 plus a 1/1 flying Inkling; one of very few token-makers, so it
  carries the go-wide package and Nita.
- **Helping Hand** / **Return to the Ranks** — only once you have multiple cheap white rares worth
  rebuying, or (for Return to the Ranks) a deck with ten-plus two-drops. He had exactly one such deck.
- **Group Project** — *"not a very good card generically"*, but a real ceiling in the scrappier decks
  as a token-maker with flashback for Aziza.
- **Ennis, Debate Moderator** — *"This card's bad. I have played it."* Only for decks with a real
  enters-ability theme (Owlin Historian, Imperious Inkmage, Practiced Scrollsmith, Stirring
  Honormancer) or multiple Conciliator's Duelists; also re-prepares Elite Interceptor.
- **Spiritcall Enthusiast** — *"not really a go-wide payoff"*, but becomes *"actually pretty good"*
  when you genuinely have the token-makers.
- **Soaring Stoneglider**, **Ascendant Dustspeaker** — named as the rest of white's flyer count when
  you are building around flying.

### Blue
- **Mathemagics** — *"the best one of the bunch"* of the control win conditions. Opening it (or
  wheeling a Wisdom of Ages) is the specific thing that would drag him into UR control against his
  own preference.
- **Wisdom of Ages** — seven mana, return all instants and sorceries. Defines the **draw-go** control
  build: it wants a cheap spell curve (Quick Study, Sleight of Hand, one- and two-mana removal). He
  rates it *"a little stronger than Divergent Equation."*
- **Divergent Equation** — XX blue, rebuy X spells. Defines the **tap-out / converge** control build:
  it wants *"higher cost, super impactful stuff, so it's enough to cast for X equals two"* — a Snarl
  Song, a Visionary's Dance, a counterspell. He credits the distinction to a listener (LameForFame in
  his Discord) and says treating the two cards identically was costing him win rate early.
- **Exhibition Tidecaller** — the in-set mill win-con. Play it around counterspells: *"instead of
  waiting till six to play it and mill 10, you wait till turn eight so you can play it, mill 10 and
  hold up a Brush Off."*
- **Stock Up** — *"a fantastic card you'll play in any blue deck."* A defensible pack-one-pick-one for
  the guest because it fits every blue deck in the format.
- **Flow State** — the mini Stock Up. *"I've really liked how flow state plays"*, and you normally get
  there on the instant-and-sorcery condition in a heavy blue deck.
- **Quick Study** — instant-speed draw two; the card that lets you hold up a counterspell and not
  waste the turn. The guest uses "does this deck want Embrace the Paradox or Quick Study?" as the
  literal test separating blue-green small from the multicolor decks.
- **Essence Scatter**, **Brush Off**, **Disdainful Stroke**, **Spell Pierce** — the counterspell suite.
  Brush Off (four mana, two less against an instant/sorcery) is *"very, very good"* and protects your
  big finish in control mirrors. Disdainful Stroke *"plays a lot better than it feels in this
  format"* because decks reliably get to large mana. **Spell Pierce is the guest's meta call of the
  format** — *"the X spells' natural predator"*, since the multicolor decks tap out for Snarl Song,
  Visionary's Dance and Arcane Omens. Samp confirms: *"I have been getting absolutely destroyed by
  Spell Pierce in the past week."*
- **Banishing Betrayal** — the first copy is fine in draw-go control (you out-card them enough that
  the tempo is worth the card disadvantage) and it is a core card in blue-green small. Multicolor
  tap-out decks want it less.
- **Run Behind** — four-mana top-or-bottom, one less against an attacker. Preferred over the sorcery-
  speed green fight spells by the guest.
- **Chase Inspiration** — the Dive Down reprint. The guest openly wishes it were Shore Up instead
  (*"I really do prefer the Royal Treatment"*) — no power, no untap.
- **Hydro-Channeler** — two-mana 1/3 that taps for blue for instants/sorceries and filters. *"A lot
  better"* in tap-out control than in draw-go, since it also fixes for converge.
- **Landscape Painter** — the flagship "don't be precious" card: a two-mana 2/1 that eventually turns
  into Mulldrifter, which you should still trade off for nothing when you need the board clean.
- **Tester of the Tangential** — *"very unimpressive looking for the first couple weeks"*, but it is
  the only sub-three-mana increment creature outside rare besides Cuboid Colony, so blue-green small
  needs it. Moves counters onto flyers.
- **Fractalize** — *"Bad. It's not a good card. There's no good number for X."* Both agree it is
  nonetheless a real sideboard/finisher option in the small decks and combos well with Tackle Artist's
  counters. The guest has lost close matches by **not** going for it.
- **Encouraging Aviator** — a way to jump Tenured Concocter, Expressive Firedancer or Tackle Artist
  past a wide board; also one of the cheap prepared spells that keeps Emeritus of Conflict online.
- **Spellbook Seeker**, **Muse's Encouragement** — marginal flyers you play once flying is a critical
  mass. Muse's Encouragement is *"way better than Wild Hypothesis"* and its surveil 2 keeps a flood-
  prone tempo deck on gas.
- **Matterbending Mage** — the Man-o'-War-style bounce body. *"Going to be at its best in this deck"*
  (blue-green small); hard to turn on the unblockable clause outside an X-spells deck.
- **Deluge Virtuoso** — the Frost Lynx variant with pseudo-prowess. *"None of them have been as good
  as Berg Strider was in Kaldheim"*, but blue-green small **is** the Berg Strider deck: you need one
  blocker gone for one turn cycle.
- **Fractal Anomaly** — never put it in a deck himself; the use case (credited to Cat Scandal in the
  Limited Level-Ups Discord) is *"more about wanting a one mana spell than it is trying to make it a
  really big creature"* — a prowess trigger for Expressive Firedancer / Deluge Virtuoso.
- **Harmonized Trio** — *"isn't really very good in this format in general"*, but rises in the
  creature-dense Prismari midrange deck where you can actually tap three bodies.
- **Wild Hypothesis** — the guest has *"not touched that card"* and says blue-green small would be
  *"a lot better"* if it were an instant.

### Black
- **Cost of Brilliance** — *"among my top commons right now"*, while conceding *"I don't think it's
  performing at that level in the 17Lands data."* Ep 59 argues it, not Shopkeeper's Bane, is the
  lynchpin of the good BG deck — and Ep 65 gives it a fond send-off as a design (Combat Tutorial's
  successor) while stating plainly it *"was not one of the best commons in this format, but it was
  very, very important to the decks that wanted it."* Keep both halves.
- **Send in the Pest** — *"the patron saint of drafts that didn't quite get there."* Two-mana discard
  plus a pest; the glue for small-game plans, Forum Necroscribe, Cauldron of Essence, Nita and The
  Dawning Archaic. Usually not the second copy — *"I think there are some exceptions."*
- **Melancholic Poet** — two-mana 2/2 with repartee draining on every targeted spell. One half of
  black's persistent reach package; he explicitly prefers it and Leech Collector to Teacher's Pest.
- **Leech Collector** — becomes prepared on your first lifegain each turn, then drains two. The other
  half of the reach package; the pair *"turn the screws"* once the opponent is at five or six.
- **Burrog Banemaker** — one-mana deathtouch with a pump. *"I'm playing the first copy in every deck
  like this"* — very good on empty boards as a mana sink.
- **Lecturing Scornmage** — one-mana 1/1 with repartee counters. *"A threat that scales with the game…
  I like this card a lot"* in decks full of removal, Cost of Brilliance and green punch spells.
- **Foolish Fate** — three-mana instant kill that drains three if you gained life. Lines up perfectly
  with the black reach plan.
- **End of the Hunt** — two-mana exile-their-best; *"really efficient."*
- **Cheerful Osteomancer / Pull from the Grave / Grave Researcher** — the black recursion package.
  Only with 15–16 creatures and genuinely important targets; better the smaller you can make the game.
  If a Grave Researcher comes early he will *"slightly build around it"* and then look for Pull from
  the Grave.
- **Postmortem Professor** — *"this card's fantastic"* in Witherbloom and Silverquill; the two-mana
  rebuy mode is an **instant**, so you can double-activate in one turn and generate three separate
  leaves-the-graveyard triggers. Also a good Lorehold splash.
- **Eternal Student** — *"a pretty high-quality card that just doesn't fit in this format."* Ep 65

- **Forum Necroscribe** — *"not performing well in the aggregate… a bit too small ball for its mana

### Red
- **Rubble Rouser** — his **favourite common in the set** and the subject of ep 58. Three-mana 1/4

  to perform well in the aggregate data."* The case for it: four toughness blocks every unmodified
  two-drop and dodges Last Gasp; the rummage smooths and gives late-game utility; the mana matters
  because the format is mana-hungry; and the **damage output is the sneakily most important part** —
  it closes grindy games, taxes their removal, and makes you live to topdeck Tome Blast or Burst
  Lightning. Midrange only — not for true aggro Lorehold, and not for a small prowess deck.
- **Tome Blast** — two mana up front, five on flashback, two damage each time. *"A card that I'm happy
  to run a couple copies of"*; four damage from one card, and reach in the burn plan.
- **Burst Lightning** — the other four-damage-for-one-card burn spell; **highly prioritised alongside
  Impractical Joke** when your deck plays Potioner's Trove, because one-mana-value plays are what make
  the Manalith turn work.
- **Impractical Joke** — one-mana deal three; the classic "Trove on three, kill something with the
  Trove mana" line.

  rest of red's cheap interaction, which is the reason UR is the best base for either control build.
  Vibrant Outburst and Burst Lightning also go face.
- **Tackle Artist** — four-mana 4/3 trample with Opus. Explicitly a **low-power-draft card**, not a

  to protect it with your Chase Inspiration, you have to wait till turn five."* Its ceiling is real
  though — it is the best Bulk Up target after Spectacular Skywhale, and it grows off Fractalize's
  base-power clause.
- **Expressive Firedancer** — two-mana 2/2 Opus prowess with double strike at five-plus mana. The other
  half of the cheese package; *"one of the best reasons to be red small on top of Tackle Artist."*
- **Monstrous Rage** — *"very, very powerful"* but *"its aggregate win rate is a lot lower than it was
  in Wilds of Eldraine"* because it doesn't fit the format's midrange/control decks. The best card in

- **Ancestral Anger** — one-mana trample-granting cantrip. He **actively tracks the wheel** for these
  in scrappy drafts and tries to accumulate a package of them with Firedancers and Tackle Artists.
- **Bulk Up** — *"the weakest card on its own, but maybe the highest ceiling of these."* He will not
  play it unless it is comboing with Monstrous Rage or Ancestral Anger — or unless he has multiple
  Tackle Artists.
- **Pigment Wrangler** — the case study in reading data correctly. Its ~51% GIH win rate *"is not
  because this is a bad card… I think this card is powerful. It just doesn't line up with the
  format"* — at five-plus mana there are better things to do, and the public over-rates medium flyers.
  A card he turns to specifically when a draft lacks power.
- **Duel Tactics** — *"doesn't line up with what the format is about"*; a sideboard card against
  one-toughness boards (pests, Stone Docent), a repartee trigger, and a way to clear blockers for a
  go-wide turn. Weaker than Dig Site Inventory for the same job.
- **Goblin Glasswright** — *"the easiest way to get a treasure in the format"*; fixes, ramps into
  Potioner's Trove or a converge spell, and is the best pairing for Mica.
- **Mica, Reader of Ruins** — high ceiling, no natural home *"unless you specifically build around
  it"*; needs artifacts, so Goblin Glasswright and (less happily) Seize the Spoils.
- **Thunderdrum Soloist** — two-mana 1/3 reach that pings on every spell, three on Opus. He warns
  against cheap creatures in draw-go and then names this as the exception; the guest lost a game
  exactly to this card's damage adding up.
- **Molten-Core Maestro** / **Charging Strifeknight** / **Stadium Tidalmage** — good-rate red cards
  that *"suffer a lot when you put them in the late game Prismari decks"* and shine in a creature-dense

- **Zealous Lorecaster** — the recursion engine that turns Snarl Song into *"Snarl Song number like
  five"* in the Divergent Equation decks.
- **Living History** — a token plus an attack pump keyed to leaves-the-graveyard; a flyer-buffing

- **Tablet of Discovery** — wanted *"a lot"* in **both** control builds alongside Potioner's Trove;

- **Emeritus of Conflict** — hard to prepare the first time, easy afterwards. Wants a critical mass of

- **Brotherhood's End** — a control/soup card, **but** pairing it with Rubble Rouser (survives it, and

## Pitfalls

- **Over-drafting Witherbloom.** The single most exploitable fact about the format: the three
  highest-picked gold commons are all BG, the lifegain curve looks coherent, and the deck *"crumbles
  so easily to interaction."* If you are in Witherbloom, build the black small-game version and assume
  you get no Bogwater Lumarets. If you are not, you can profitably ignore the whole college.
- **Being precious with prepared creatures.** The format's main play mistake. You must trade off a
  Landscape Painter or an Elite Interceptor for nothing when the board demands it. Secure board parity
  and keep your life total high **first** — that is what buys you the option to keep a valuable
  prepared creature later.
- **Filling out a draw-go control curve with random creatures.** A dinky two-drop hands your opponent a
  target for the removal spell that would otherwise be stranded in their hand. If your plan is to
  counter or burn their early creatures, actually commit to it.
- **Reading aggregate win rate as power.** Pigment Wrangler, Monstrous Rage, Bulk Up, The Dawning
  Archaic and Forum Necroscribe all have poor aggregate numbers and real ceilings. *"The aggregate win
  rate data is really not necessarily an indication of how powerful a card is… it is telling a story
  about the format."*
- **Judging the format by average game length.** He rejects the metric outright — it mostly tracks
  power level, and slow games in any format skew it. Khans of Tarkir felt slow and was won by WB
  aggro; original Strixhaven had huge late-game mirrors and was won by white curve-out.
- **Building five-colour because a card told you to.** Converge worked, but Ep 65's lesson is that the
  gold-card density already pushes you into multiple colours — *"I just don't think you need more than
  like one card in a set that specifically tells you to jam as many colors as possible."* Don't play
  off-colour basics purely to hit converge; let the Trove and the odd dual do it incidentally.
- **Treating the two big blue rebuy spells as interchangeable.** Wisdom of Ages wants a cheap curve;
  Divergent Equation wants expensive, impactful spells. He says conflating them was costing him games.
- **Sequencing lands on autopilot.** The format is mana-hungry (play them), but Rubble Rouser wants a
  spare card to rummage and Send in the Pest punishes a full hand. Clock whether the opponent is on
  black before deciding.
- **Drafting against your own strengths.** Ep 64's framing: know whether this draft is for learning or
  for winning. If you know you dislike and misplay the control decks, bias away from them in the early
  picks — the table will force you in when it is genuinely open, and it will be obvious.

## Deep dives

Only one SOS episode actually sustains its namesake card for most of its runtime. The other five
card-titled episodes (Shopkeeper's Bane, Potioner's Trove, Wisdom of Ages, Quandrix Charm, Tackle
Artist) use the card as a framing device and spend their runtime on archetype or format theory —
their content is in the sections above, not here.

### Ep 58 — Rubble Rouser: the four-toughness breakpoint and mana output as a win condition
A genuine card dissection: Samp walks the card part by part — stat line, rummage, mana ability, damage
output, graveyard cost — and each part carries a general lesson.

1. **Stat line.** He proposes the **four-toughness breakpoint** as a better heuristic than the vanilla
   test: four toughness blocks every unmodified two-drop ever printed (Tyrok's Aether Revolt 4/1
   excepted) and dodges Last Gasp, so a card up the curve with four toughness cannot be traded at a
   mana advantage. This is *"pretty foundational to how I evaluate cards up the curve"*, and he notes
   he had not said it on the podcast before.
2. **Rummage.** Smooths draws, gives a topdecked copy late-game utility, and matters specifically for
   midrange decks that don't know which role they will play.
3. **Mana.** Citing a Sierkovitz chart from Lord of the Rings limited on win rate as a function of
   mana spent versus your opponent, he argues Rubble Rouser is **not** about a specific curve skip
   (the way Llanowar Elves is) but about **total mana output across the game** — which matters more
   in SOS than in most formats because flashback and prepared mean you never run out of sinks.
4. **Damage.** The part he calls *"sneakily maybe the most important."* Extended comparison to
   Ill-Gotten Inheritance in Ravnica Allegiance: the value is that your opponent must **play as though
   they are several life lower than they are**, which constrains their attacks and forces their removal
   at a card that looks defensive. Combined with Tome Blast and Burst Lightning, it makes you live to
   just draw the kill from seven or eight.
5. **Cost.** You need graveyard fuel, which is nearly free in Lorehold (Pursue the Past, Terramorphic
   Expanse, Hop to It, Muse's Encouragement) — *"I don't think you really need to build around Rubble
   Rouser in terms of stocking your graveyard."*

The verdict is hedged and stays hedged: a **midrange** card only, wrong in true-aggro Lorehold, right
in the Prismari decks that want Visionary's Dance. Ep 65 makes it his favourite common in the set,
still granting that Colossus of the Blood Age was *"probably even with Rubble Rouser"* on raw card quality.
